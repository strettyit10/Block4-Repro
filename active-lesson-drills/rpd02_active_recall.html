Q1. A research team generates a mouse embryo in which the ureteric bud is surgically ablated on the right side at the equivalent of human gestational week 5, while the left side is left intact. At birth, imaging on the right side would MOST likely show which of the following?
A. A normal-sized right kidney with a duplicated ureter
B. A dysplastic right kidney with cystic remnants and no ureter
C. Complete absence of the right kidney and right ureter
D. A pelvic right kidney with variant iliac blood supply
E. A horseshoe-shaped kidney with bilateral ureters
C — Complete absence of the right kidney and right ureter (unilateral renal agenesis).
Without a ureteric bud, there is no WNT9B/FGF2 signal to induce the metanephric blastema, so no nephrons condense. Reciprocal induction fails on that side → no collecting system AND no functional nephrons. The contralateral side compensates (hypertrophy); unilateral disease is usually compatible with life. Bilateral bud failure → bilateral renal agenesis → Potter sequence (oligohydramnios → pulmonary hypoplasia).
Q2. A neonate dies shortly after birth from respiratory failure. Autopsy reveals flattened facies, clubbed feet, and severely hypoplastic lungs. Prenatal ultrasound had shown oligohydramnios and bilateral absent kidneys. Which of the following BEST explains the cause of death?
A. Acute renal failure from bilateral renal agenesis
B. Uremia-induced encephalopathy
C. Pulmonary hypoplasia due to absent fetal urine → oligohydramnios → failed lung distension
D. Pulmonary surfactant deficiency from premature birth
E. Cardiac defects from failed neural crest migration
C — Pulmonary hypoplasia from oligohydramnios (Potter sequence).
The placenta filters fetal blood during gestation, so the fetus does not die of uremia. After ~20 weeks, amniotic fluid comes almost entirely from fetal urine; without kidneys, oligohydramnios develops → mechanical compression (Potter facies, limb deformities) AND failed amniotic-fluid-driven lung distension → pulmonary hypoplasia = actual cause of neonatal death.
Q3. A 16-year-old girl presents with primary amenorrhea. Pelvic imaging reveals a bicornuate uterus, and renal ultrasound shows an absent right kidney. Which single embryologic principle BEST explains the co-occurrence of these two findings?
A. Failure of neural crest migration
B. Failed urorectal septum fusion
C. Abnormal SRY translocation
D. The mesonephric duct scaffolds paramesonephric descent and sprouts the ureteric bud — one disruption, two findings
E. Failed sinovaginal node fusion
D — The mesonephric (Wolffian) duct is the shared scaffold.
The paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts descend alongside the mesonephric ducts; partial fusion failure produces a bicornuate uterus. The ureteric bud sprouts from the caudal mesonephric duct; its failure → unilateral renal agenesis. A single Wolffian/paramesonephric disruption can produce both defects on the same side. Always order a renal ultrasound when a Müllerian anomaly is identified.
Q4. A phenotypically male newborn is undergoing surgery for bilateral non-palpable testes. The surgeon unexpectedly finds a uterus and two fallopian tubes. Karyotype is 46,XY; testosterone, DHT, and LH are all normal for age. Which embryologic event failed?
A. Leydig cells failed to produce testosterone
B. Neural crest migration into the distal cloaca failed
C. Sertoli cells failed to produce MIS, or target tissues lack the MIS receptor
D. The metanephric blastema failed to receive WNT9B
E. 5α-reductase activity was absent in target tissues
C — Persistent Müllerian Duct Syndrome (PMDS).
Sertoli cells normally produce MIS (AMH), which triggers regression of the paramesonephric ducts at week 7–8. If MIS is absent (AMH gene mutation) or the MIS type II receptor is nonfunctional, Müllerian structures persist despite otherwise normal male development (testosterone from Leydig → Wolffian SEED; DHT → external male genitalia). Retained Müllerian structures often tether the testes intra-abdominally → cryptorchidism.
Q5. A 14-year-old phenotypically female patient presents with primary amenorrhea. Exam reveals Tanner IV breasts, sparse pubic hair, and a short blind-ending vagina. Imaging shows no uterus or fallopian tubes; bilateral inguinal masses are present. Karyotype is 46,XY. Which ONE of the following best explains both the absent uterus AND the breast development?
A. Functional Sertoli cells (MIS active) AND functional aromatase converting testosterone to estradiol
B. Failed SRY signaling with streak gonads producing endogenous estrogen
C. Defective 5α-reductase with normal aromatase
D. 21-hydroxylase deficiency with virilization
E. Functional Müllerian ducts with exogenous androgen exposure
A — Complete Androgen Insensitivity (CAIS).
Testes form normally (SRY present). Sertoli cells make MIS → Müllerian regression → no uterus, no upper vagina. Leydig cells produce testosterone normally or high, but the mutant androgen receptor cannot respond → Wolffian ducts regress and external genitalia default to female. Peripheral aromatase converts the high circulating testosterone to estradiol → breast development at puberty. Sparse pubic/axillary hair reflects lost androgen receptor signaling. Inguinal masses = undescended testes.
Q6. A 46,XY infant is born with ambiguous, predominantly female-appearing external genitalia: a small phallus, penoscrotal hypospadias, labial fusion, and a blind-ending vagina. Testosterone level is high-normal and LH is mildly elevated. By late adolescence, the patient has developed increased muscle mass, a deepened voice, and growth of the phallus, but no breast development. What lab finding would confirm the diagnosis?
A. Elevated 17-hydroxyprogesterone
B. Elevated testosterone-to-DHT ratio
C. Karyotype showing 47,XXY
D. Elevated FSH with low inhibin B
E. Absent response to exogenous HCG stimulation
B — Elevated testosterone-to-DHT ratio (5α-reductase deficiency).
Autosomal recessive loss of 5α-reductase prevents T → DHT conversion in target tissues. Internal Wolffian ducts (T-dependent) form normally; external genitalia (DHT-dependent) do not masculinize until the pubertal T surge directly overwhelms the block. Classic "female at birth, male at puberty"; androgen receptor works (hence muscle/phallus growth, no breast development).
Q7. A 2-week-old 46,XX infant with ambiguous genitalia presents with vomiting, poor feeding, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypotension. Serum 17-hydroxyprogesterone is markedly elevated. Which of the following is the MOST appropriate immediate management?
A. Testosterone replacement therapy to masculinize genitalia
B. Hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone + IV fluids/glucose
C. Surgical gonadectomy
D. Methylprednisolone taper only
E. Spironolactone and potassium restriction
B — Hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone + IV fluids.
This is classic salt-wasting 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Hydrocortisone replaces cortisol AND suppresses ACTH (reducing androgen shunting). Fludrocortisone replaces aldosterone to correct the salt-wasting (hyponatremia, hyperkalemia). Giving testosterone (A) to a virilized 46,XX would worsen virilization. Gonadectomy (C) has no role — gonads are ovaries. Spironolactone (E) would further deplete aldosterone activity.
Q8. A 15-year-old girl is evaluated for short stature, primary amenorrhea, webbed neck, and shield chest. Echocardiography shows a bicuspid aortic valve with mild coarctation. Renal ultrasound reveals a horseshoe kidney. Which embryologic mechanism BEST unifies the renal finding with the rest of her presentation?
A. Failure of neural crest migration into the distal cloaca
B. Fusion of the two metanephric blastemas across the midline, trapped under the IMA during ascent
C. Failed reciprocal induction between the ureteric bud and metanephric blastema
D. Failure of the urorectal septum to fuse with the cloacal membrane
E. Abnormal rotation of the kidneys with failure of the nephrogenic cord
B — Midline fusion of the metanephric blastemas, caught under the inferior mesenteric artery during ascent.
The patient has Turner syndrome (45,XO): short stature (SHOX haploinsufficiency), streak gonads with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, webbed neck (cystic hygroma), bicuspid aortic valve + coarctation, and horseshoe kidney. The horseshoe is formed when the lower poles fuse across the midline; the isthmus catches on the IMA → the kidney sits low at L3–L5. Turner patients should have lifelong cardiac monitoring for aortic dissection and renal monitoring for reflux/UTIs.
