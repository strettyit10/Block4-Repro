Q1. A 28-year-old man with a history of unilateral cryptorchidism that underwent orchidopexy at age 8 presents with infertility. Semen analysis shows azoospermia. Labs: testosterone 420 ng/dL (normal), LH 9 mIU/mL (normal-high), FSH 28 mIU/mL (high), inhibin B 20 pg/mL (low). Which ONE cell population is MOST directly responsible for the pattern of isolated FSH elevation?
A. Leydig cells — heat-damaged → loss of testosterone → ↑ LH
B. Sertoli cells — heat-damaged → loss of inhibin B → ↑ FSH
C. Spermatogonia — directly suppress LH by paracrine signaling
D. Myoid cells around the tubule produce inhibin B
E. Pituitary gonadotrophs are intrinsically damaged by heat
B — Heat-damaged Sertoli cells → ↓ inhibin B → loss of selective FSH negative feedback.
Leydig cells are heat-RESISTANT, so testosterone stays normal and LH is only mildly elevated. Sertoli cells (and germ cells) are heat-SENSITIVE: damage → ↓ inhibin B, which is the specific negative-feedback inhibitor of FSH → FSH rises in isolation. Late orchidopexy (age 8) did not prevent the damage — intervention before age 1 preserves more fertility. Cancer surveillance is still required (↑ seminoma risk persists).
Q2. A 35-year-old man presents with a left-sided scrotal mass that feels like a "bag of worms" and decompresses when he lies supine. Which venous drainage anomaly BEST explains the left-sided predominance of this finding?
A. The left testicular vein drains into the IVC at an acute angle
B. The right testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at a right angle
C. The left testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at a 90° angle → ↑ backpressure
D. Left-sided varicoceles are caused by congenital absence of the pampiniform plexus
E. The cremasteric artery is shorter on the left
C — Left testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at 90° → ↑ backpressure → dilation of the pampiniform plexus.
~90% of varicoceles are left-sided for this reason. The RIGHT testicular vein drains directly into the IVC at an acute angle (less resistance). A new RIGHT-sided varicocele — or a left varicocele that does NOT decompress supine — should raise concern for renal cell carcinoma invading the renal vein/IVC and occluding drainage.
Q3. A pathologist examines a prostate biopsy and reports small, crowded glands with prominent nucleoli and a single layer of cells (no basal layer identified on p63 or HMWCK staining). Corpora amylacea are visible in adjacent benign-appearing glands. Which of the following BEST explains the pathologist's conclusion?
A. BPH — the missing basal layer is a hyperplasia artifact
B. Prostatitis — acute inflammation obscures the basal cells
C. Prostatic adenocarcinoma — loss of the basal cell layer supports invasion
D. Granulomatous prostatitis after BCG therapy
E. Normal prostatic histology
C — Prostate adenocarcinoma. Loss of the basal cell layer is a hallmark of invasion.
Normal and benign (BPH) prostatic glands have a TWO-cell epithelium — inner luminal secretory + outer basal. Pathologists confirm the basal layer with p63 or HMWCK (high-molecular-weight cytokeratin). Absence of basal cells + small crowded glands + prominent nucleoli → adenocarcinoma. Cancer typically arises in the PERIPHERAL zone (vs BPH in the TRANSITION zone). Corpora amylacea are age-related benign inclusions — they sit in neighboring glands but don't confer malignancy.
Q4. A 42-year-old man with infertility, bilateral absence of the vas deferens on palpation, and an ejaculate volume of 0.4 mL with ABSENT fructose undergoes genetic testing. Which mutation is MOST likely, and what is the embryologic link explaining the low volume and absent fructose?
A. AR mutation — Wolffian derivatives never form without androgen signaling
B. SRY mutation — testes don't form, so no ducts form
C. CFTR mutation — CBAVD with associated hypoplastic seminal vesicles (Wolffian-duct derivatives)
D. 5α-reductase deficiency — external genitalia defects but normal ducts
E. Kallmann syndrome — hypothalamic GnRH deficiency
C — CFTR mutation → Congenital Bilateral Absence of the Vas Deferens (CBAVD).
CFTR mutations cause fetal obstruction/atresia of the developing vas deferens. Because the vas and seminal vesicles are both derived from the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct, seminal vesicles are often absent or hypoplastic as well. Seminal vesicles contribute ~70% of ejaculate volume AND are the sole source of fructose — so CBAVD classically presents with low-volume, fructose-negative azoospermic ejaculate. Leydig function (and testosterone) is preserved. Management: testicular sperm extraction + ICSI; genetic counseling for the partner.
Q5. A 17-year-old boy presents to the ED with sudden, severe right-sided testicular pain that began 3 hours ago while playing basketball. On exam, the right testis is high-riding and transverse, the cremasteric reflex is absent, and elevation of the scrotum does NOT relieve pain. Doppler US shows absent flow to the right testis. What is the MOST appropriate next step?
A. Start ceftriaxone + doxycycline empirically
B. Schedule outpatient urology follow-up within 48 hours
C. Emergent surgical detorsion with bilateral orchiopexy
D. Give IV antibiotics and observe for 24 hours
E. MRI pelvis to confirm the diagnosis
C — Emergent surgical detorsion + bilateral orchiopexy.
Classic testicular torsion: sudden onset, high-riding/transverse testis, absent cremasteric reflex, negative Prehn sign (elevation does NOT help), and absent Doppler flow. This is a surgical emergency — the 6-hour window is key; delay risks ischemic infarction. Bilateral orchiopexy is done because the predisposing "bell-clapper deformity" is typically bilateral. Epididymitis (A) would have positive Prehn, preserved cremasteric reflex, and INCREASED Doppler flow.
Q6. A pathologist identifies a male reproductive duct whose luminal epithelium has a characteristic "scalloped" or "toothed" appearance from alternating tall ciliated and short non-ciliated cells. What structure is this, and what is its embryologic origin?
A. Rete testis; intermediate mesoderm
B. Epididymis; mesonephric duct
C. Efferent ductules; mesonephric tubules
D. Vas deferens; mesonephric duct
E. Seminal vesicle; urogenital sinus
C — Efferent ductules, derived from mesonephric TUBULES.
The "scalloped" or "toothed" border is the pathognomonic visual feature of efferent ductules. The tall ciliated cells move sperm toward the epididymis; the short non-ciliated cells reabsorb ~90% of testicular fluid, concentrating sperm. The mesonephric-tubule origin (same as kidney tubules) is why they retain strong fluid-resorptive capacity. The vas deferens and epididymis come from the mesonephric DUCT (not tubules) and have smooth pseudostratified columnar epithelium with stereocilia.
Q7. A 68-year-old man with BPH on tamsulosin and diabetic autonomic neuropathy reports that his ejaculate volume has dropped to almost nothing, and his post-coital urine is cloudy. Which structure and mechanism BEST explain this finding?
A. External urethral sphincter dysfunction → urinary incontinence
B. Internal urethral sphincter fails to close → retrograde flow of semen into the bladder
C. Seminal vesicles atrophy from chronic α1 blockade
D. Prostatic ductal obstruction from BPH
E. Pelvic floor weakness from aging
B — Retrograde ejaculation — internal urethral sphincter fails to close during emission.
During normal emission, sympathetic α1 tone closes the internal urethral sphincter at the bladder neck, directing semen forward. Tamsulosin (selective α1A antagonist) blocks this — AND diabetic autonomic neuropathy further impairs sympathetic function — so semen flows backward into the bladder. Orgasm is preserved; post-coital urinalysis shows sperm, confirming the diagnosis. The external urethral sphincter (skeletal, pudendal-controlled) is unaffected → urinary continence is preserved.
Q8. A 30-year-old man is brought to the ED after a motorcycle accident with a displaced pelvic fracture. He is unable to void, and blood is visible at the urethral meatus. DRE reveals a high-riding prostate. The urology resident wants to place a Foley catheter. What is the next appropriate step, and which urethral segment is MOST likely disrupted?
A. Place the Foley immediately; the membranous urethra is resilient to trauma
B. Obtain a retrograde urethrogram; the membranous urethra is most vulnerable to pelvic fractures
C. CT cystogram; the prostatic urethra usually ruptures in pelvic trauma
D. Suprapubic cystostomy only; never image urethra in trauma
E. Attempt a small-caliber catheter under fluoroscopic guidance
B — Retrograde urethrogram; pelvic fractures classically disrupt the MEMBRANOUS urethra.
The membranous urethra is the SHORTEST, narrowest, and most fixed segment — it traverses the urogenital diaphragm, so pelvic fractures that disrupt the diaphragm shear it. Red-flag signs are present: blood at meatus + high-riding prostate + inability to void. Blind catheterization risks converting a partial tear into a complete one. Always do retrograde urethrogram first; if urgent bladder drainage is needed, use suprapubic cystostomy. Contrast this with straddle injury (perineum against a bike crossbar) → bulbous urethra rupture + "butterfly" perineal bruising.
