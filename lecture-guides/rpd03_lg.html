▼ 🫐 Gross Organization of the Ovary

⭐ The ovary is both a primary sex organ (gonad) and a mixed endocrine-exocrine gland. It makes and releases oocytes (exocrine) and secretes estrogen and progesterone (endocrine).

🔬 Three Histologic Zones

ZoneHistologyClinical / HY point
Germinal epithelium (surface)Simple cuboidal covering; overlies tunica albuginea (dense CT capsule)Most ovarian cancers arise from germinal (surface) epithelium — so "epithelial ovarian cancer" is the dominant subtype (serous carcinoma most common)
CortexConnective-tissue stroma housing follicles at every stage of developmentSite of folliculogenesis, corpus luteum, corpus albicans scars
MedullaLoose CT with blood vessels, lymphatics, nervesNo follicles here — tumors in the medulla are usually stromal/germ-cell rather than epithelial
⚡ HY — Two Exam Buzzphrases:
  • "Mass arising from surface epithelium" → epithelial ovarian cancer (serous, mucinous, endometrioid, clear-cell).
  • All oocytes are born with you — no new oocytes form after birth; you lose them from ~7 million at 20 weeks gestation, to ~1–2 million at birth, to ~400,000 at puberty, to zero at menopause.
⚠️ TRAP: "Germ cells originate in the ovary" ▼
  • FALSE. Primordial germ cells (PGCs) originate in the endoderm of the yolk sac wall, then migrate along the hindgut dorsal mesentery to seed the genital ridge at week 5. They are not ovarian in origin — this is why extragonadal germ-cell tumors can arise along the midline (retroperitoneum, mediastinum, sacrococcyx).

▼ 💤 Primordial Follicle — The Lifetime Dormant Pool

⭐ Every oocyte a woman will ever have is stored as a primordial follicle. These follicles are arrested in prophase I of meiosis and remain dormant until recruited — some for 40+ years.

📘 Structure & State

  • Oocyte: primary oocyte, frozen in prophase I (diplotene) of meiosis since fetal life
  • Follicular cells: a single layer of squamous (flat) cells surrounding the oocyte
  • Location: outer cortex, just beneath the tunica albuginea
  • At birth: ALL ovarian follicles are at this stage
  • FSH receptors: not yet expressed — these cells are not responsive to gonadotropins
Fetal life → ~7 million oogonia at 20 weeks
↓ atresia
Birth: ~1–2 million primary oocytes, all in primordial follicles
↓ continuous atresia (even pre-puberty)
Puberty: ~400,000 remaining
↓
Menopause: ~0 follicles remaining

▼ 🧱 Primary Follicle — Unilaminar & Multilaminar Stages

⭐ At puberty, FSH from the anterior pituitary recruits a cohort of primordial follicles every cycle (~15–20). The flat follicular cells become cuboidal/columnar granulosa cells — this is the moment the follicle enters active development.

🟦 Unilaminar Primary Follicle

  • Primary oocyte still arrested in prophase I
  • Single layer of cuboidal granulosa cells (upregulated FSH receptors)
  • Surrounded by a distinct basal lamina (basement membrane)
  • FSH-responsive: granulosa cells begin proliferating

🟦🟦 Multilaminar Primary Follicle

  • Granulosa cells undergo hyperplasia & hypertrophy → multiple layers
  • Zona pellucida forms — a glycoprotein coat between the oocyte and granulosa cells; composed of ZP1, ZP2, ZP3, synthesized cooperatively by both oocyte and granulosa cells
    • ZP3 is the sperm receptor — mutations here cause fertilization failure
  • Stromal cells outside the basal lamina begin differentiating into theca interna — the steroidogenic cell layer
  • Oocyte-granulosa communication via gap junctions across microvilli that traverse the zona pellucida
⚡ HY — Zona pellucida components:
  • ZP3 = sperm-binding receptor. Key role in the acrosome reaction.
  • Cortical reaction after fertilization modifies ZP2/3 → block to polyspermy ("zona block").
⚠️ TRAP: "FSH Acts on the Oocyte" ▼
  • FSH receptors are on granulosa cells, NOT the oocyte. The oocyte never directly sees FSH.
  • Follicular recruitment is really "granulosa-cell proliferation driven by FSH."

▼ 💧 Secondary (Antral) Follicle — Two Thecas, One Antrum

⭐ The secondary follicle is defined by the appearance of a fluid-filled antrum containing liquor folliculi (hyaluronic-acid rich) and oocyte meiosis inhibitor (OMI). This is the stage of dominant-follicle selection.

🔷 Key Features

  • Antrum (fluid cavity) forms from coalescence of small vacuoles (Call–Exner bodies) among granulosa cells
  • Liquor folliculi — hyaluronic-acid-rich fluid; contains estrogens, androgens, and OMI (keeps oocyte arrested in prophase I)
  • Theca interna — differentiated steroidogenic cells outside the basal lamina
    • Express LH receptors
    • Convert cholesterol → androstenedione (using desmolase + 17α-hydroxylase)
  • Theca externa — outer fibrous connective-tissue layer; no hormone production

⚡ Two-Cell, Two-Gonadotropin Theory

Estrogen synthesis requires a cooperative relay between theca and granulosa cells — neither cell type can make estrogen alone. This is the high-yield heart of the lecture:

Theca interna (LH-responsive)
cholesterol → desmolase → pregnenolone → 17α-OH → androstenedione
↓ diffuses across basal lamina
Granulosa cells (FSH-responsive)
androstenedione → aromatase (CYP19) → estradiol
🧠 Mnemonic: "Theca gives LH-androgen, Granulosa gives FSH-estrogen"
▼
  • Theca → Testosterone precursor (androstenedione), driven by LH (two "T" cells, "L" for LH)
  • Granulosa → Estradiol, driven by FSH (FSH + aromatase)
  • Aromatase is a drug target: anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane all block it to reduce estrogen (breast cancer Rx, ovulation induction).
⚡ Clinical Tie-Ins:
  • Granulosa-cell tumor (sex-cord stromal tumor): oversecretes estrogen → postmenopausal bleeding, endometrial hyperplasia, precocious puberty in girls; classic histology shows Call–Exner bodies.
  • Theca-cell tumor (thecoma / Sertoli-Leydig analog): can oversecrete androgens → virilization.
  • PCOS: ↑ LH:FSH ratio → excess theca androgens, insufficient aromatization → hyperandrogenism + anovulation.
  • Aromatase deficiency in pregnancy → maternal AND fetal virilization (fetal androgens can't be converted to estrogens in placenta).
⚠️ TRAP: Who Has Which Receptor? ▼
  • Granulosa cells: FSH receptors (always); LH receptors only after the LH surge / luteinization.
  • Theca interna: LH receptors (always).
  • Common trap: assuming granulosa have LH receptors from the start. They only acquire LH responsiveness once FSH + estradiol induce them — enabling the LH surge to trigger ovulation.

▼ 🏆 Graafian (Mature / Tertiary) Follicle

⭐ By mid-cycle, ONE follicle from the recruited cohort emerges as dominant. It has the largest antrum, the highest FSH-receptor density, the highest aromatase activity, and makes the most estradiol. All others undergo atresia.

🔍 Key Histologic Structures

  • Eccentrically-located oocyte — pushed to one side of the enlarged antrum
  • Cumulus oophorus — a mound of granulosa cells that anchors the oocyte to the follicular wall via a pedicle
  • Corona radiata — innermost single layer of granulosa cells directly adjacent to the oocyte; released with the oocyte at ovulation
  • Zona pellucida — still present between oocyte and corona radiata
  • Antrum — markedly enlarged, filled with liquor folliculi
  • Theca interna + externa — maintained around the basal lamina
🧠 Layers Outside → In (at ovulation)
▼

From ovary to oocyte plasma membrane:

  • Theca externa → Theca interna → Basal lamina → Granulosa (membrana granulosa) → Antrum → Cumulus oophorus → Corona radiata → Zona pellucida → Oocyte membrane
  • At ovulation, sperm must burrow through corona radiata (with hyaluronidase) and zona pellucida (via acrosomal enzymes binding ZP3).

▼ 💥 Ovulation & Release of Meiotic Arrest

⭐ Rising estradiol (from the dominant Graafian follicle) switches hypothalamic feedback from negative to positive, triggering the LH surge. LH breaks through OMI, resumes meiosis I, and drives follicular rupture.

🧭 Sequence of Events Around Ovulation

Rising estradiol (>200 pg/mL for ≥48 h) → positive feedback on anterior pituitary
↓
LH surge (massive release ~24 h before ovulation)
↓
LH overrides OMI → oocyte completes meiosis I → becomes a secondary oocyte + first polar body
↓
Secondary oocyte immediately enters meiosis II → arrests again at metaphase II
↓
Follicular wall thins, collagenases digest tunica albuginea → rupture @ ~day 14
↓
Oocyte + corona radiata released into peritoneal cavity; swept up by fimbriae of the oviduct
⚡ Meiosis Stops & Starts:
  • Primordial follicle → arrest in prophase I since fetal life
  • LH surge → completes meiosis I (~hours before ovulation)
  • Secondary oocyte → arrest in metaphase II at ovulation
  • Meiosis II completes only if fertilization occurs (sperm entry releases arrest)
🧠 Signs of Ovulation (Clinical)
▼
  • Mittelschmerz — unilateral lower abdominal pain at mid-cycle from peritoneal irritation by follicular fluid/blood.
  • Basal body temp rises by ~0.5°F post-ovulation (progesterone thermogenic effect).
  • Cervical mucus becomes thin, stretchy ("spinnbarkeit") — estrogen effect facilitating sperm transit.
  • Urine LH kits detect the surge — positive test predicts ovulation in ~24–36 h.
⚠️ TRAP: "Ovulation Releases a Mature Egg Ready for Fertilization" ▼
  • The oocyte ovulated is a secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II — it is NOT a fully mature haploid egg (n) until sperm triggers completion of meiosis II.
  • This is why in vitro oocytes for IVF are typically described as "M-II arrested."

▼ 🌕 Corpus Luteum & Corpus Albicans

⭐ After ovulation, the ruptured follicle transforms into the corpus luteum ("yellow body") — a temporary endocrine gland that primarily secretes progesterone to support a potential pregnancy.

🟡 Corpus Luteum — The Post-Ovulation Endocrine Gland

Luteinization is the key transformation — granulosa and theca cells enlarge, lipidize (hence yellow), and switch their hormone output:

Original cellLuteal descendantPrimary hormone output
Granulosa (FSH + aromatase → estradiol)Granulosa-lutein cellsProgesterone (+ some estradiol)
Theca interna (LH → androstenedione)Theca-lutein cellsEstradiol (via granulosa aromatase crosstalk) + androgens
⚡ High-Yield — Corpus Luteum Life Cycle:
  • Maintenance requires LH (or hCG if pregnant).
  • If no fertilization → LH drops due to progesterone negative feedback → corpus luteum degenerates by day 24–28 → corpus albicans (white CT scar) forms.
  • If fertilization occurs → syncytiotrophoblast releases hCG (mimics LH) → rescues corpus luteum → progesterone production continues until ~weeks 8–10.
  • Luteal–placental shift ≈ week 8–10: placenta takes over progesterone production; corpus luteum regresses. Progesterone supplementation may be needed if shift occurs late.

⚪ Corpus Albicans

  • White fibrous scar in the ovarian cortex — remnant of an involuted corpus luteum
  • Autolysis of luteal cells + invasion by fibroblasts → dense collagenous tissue
  • Multiple corpora albicantes accumulate with age and are visible on histology / ultrasound of the ovary
⚠️ TRAP: Theca-Lutein Cysts in Molar Pregnancy ▼
  • Very high hCG (as in complete hydatidiform mole, choriocarcinoma, or multiple gestations) causes bilateral theca-lutein cysts — large ovaries filled with fluid-filled follicles.
  • They regress once hCG drops to normal. Key stem finding: "bilaterally enlarged cystic ovaries" + elevated β-hCG.

📚 Step 1 Summary
  • Primordial → Primary (uni → multi) → Secondary (antrum + theca) → Graafian → ovulation → Corpus luteum → Corpus albicans.
  • Two-cell theory: LH on theca → androstenedione; FSH on granulosa → aromatase → estradiol.
  • Oocyte meiotic arrests: Prophase I (birth → LH surge), Metaphase II (ovulation → fertilization).
  • Corpus luteum makes PROGESTERONE; rescued by hCG if pregnancy occurs.
  • Epithelial ovarian cancers arise from surface (germinal) epithelium — most common ovarian malignancy.

🧠 Testing Your Understanding!

Testing Your Understanding — LO 1, Question 1

A 29-year-old woman undergoing workup for infertility is found to have a homozygous loss-of-function mutation in the aromatase gene (CYP19A1). Predict the hormonal, follicular, and clinical consequences of this defect, and explain why both the patient AND her mother experienced virilization during the patient's prenatal life.

Consider: two-cell theory, theca-to-granulosa relay, placental aromatase, maternal-fetal hormone crosstalk

✓ Answer: Aromatase deficiency blocks conversion of androgens to estrogens in granulosa cells — and in the placenta during pregnancy.

In the ovary, granulosa cells cannot convert theca-derived androstenedione to estradiol → low estradiol, elevated androgens in the follicle. Consequences: (1) failure of follicular maturation because estrogen is required for granulosa-cell proliferation and LH receptor upregulation → anovulation and infertility; (2) no positive estradiol feedback → no LH surge; (3) high FSH (no negative feedback); (4) ovarian cysts from chronic stimulation. During fetal life, placental aromatase normally converts fetal adrenal androgens (DHEA-S → T) to estrogens; without it, androgens cross back to the mother → maternal virilization during pregnancy that resolves post-partum, and 46,XX virilization of the fetus. At puberty: delayed secondary sex characteristics, tall stature (estrogen closes growth plates), and osteoporosis risk. Consider: why would treatment include low-dose estrogen replacement? (To close growth plates, develop secondary sex characteristics, and prevent osteoporosis.)

Testing Your Understanding — LO 1, Question 2

A pathologist reviews an ovarian biopsy from a postmenopausal woman with new-onset vaginal bleeding. Microscopy shows solid and tubular arrangements of neoplastic cells with scattered Call–Exner bodies (small rosettes of cells around eosinophilic fluid). Identify the tumor, explain the hormone it most likely secretes, and predict the endometrial finding.

Consider: granulosa-cell embryology, Call–Exner bodies, estrogen-driven endometrial pathology

✓ Answer: Adult granulosa-cell tumor (sex-cord stromal tumor) secreting estrogen → endometrial hyperplasia → bleeding.

Granulosa cells are the FSH-responsive / aromatase-containing cells of the follicle; tumors retain their estrogen-producing capacity. Call–Exner bodies — small follicle-like rosettes of granulosa cells around eosinophilic, Call-Exner fluid — are pathognomonic on histology. Postmenopausally, estrogen secretion causes endometrial hyperplasia and, with time, ↑ risk of endometrial adenocarcinoma; bleeding is the presenting sign. In children, granulosa-cell tumors cause precocious puberty. Treatment: surgical resection; long-term surveillance for late recurrence. Consider: how would a theca-cell (thecoma) or Sertoli-Leydig tumor differ? (Thecoma also makes estrogen but without Call-Exner bodies; Sertoli-Leydig tumors typically cause virilization via androgens.)

▼ 🎺 Overview — Four Regions of the Oviduct

⭐ The fallopian tubes (oviducts / uterine tubes) are paired muscular tubes that capture the ovulated oocyte, host fertilization, and transport the early embryo to the uterus. They are divided into four anatomic segments — from ovary to uterus.

📐 Four Segments (Lateral → Medial)

Ovary → Infundibulum (with fimbriae) → Ampulla (widest segment) → Isthmus (narrowest) → Intramural (pars interstitialis) → Uterus
SegmentShape / FeatureClinical / HY point
InfundibulumTrumpet-shaped opening into peritoneum; fringed by fimbriaeFimbriae "sweep" the ovulated oocyte into the tube via ciliary currents
AmpullaLongest, most dilated segment; complex mucosal folds (highly convoluted)Site of fertilization (~99% of the time); most common site of ectopic pregnancy (~70–80%)
IsthmusShortest, narrowest segment just before entering the uterusThick muscular wall; common site of tubal ligation (sterilization)
Pars interstitialis (intramural)Segment that traverses the uterine wall (myometrium)Interstitial ectopic pregnancy is rare but catastrophic — high risk of hemorrhage
🧠 Mnemonic: "I Am Ignored Inside" (IAII)
▼

Segments from lateral (ovary side) to medial (uterus side):

  • Infundibulum
  • Ampulla (fertilization + ectopic site)
  • Isthmus (tubal ligation site)
  • Intramural / pars interstitialis

▼ 🔬 Histologic Layers — Mucosa · Muscularis · Serosa

⭐ Like most tubular organs, the oviduct has three layers from lumen outward: mucosa, muscularis, serosa. The mucosa is highly folded (especially in the ampulla) and lined by a characteristic two-cell epithelium.

🔹 Mucosa

  • Epithelium: simple columnar
  • Two epithelial cell types (see below)
  • Mucosal folds (plicae) are most prominent in the ampulla — this is why the ampulla looks "labyrinthine" on histology
  • Underlying lamina propria is loose CT (no submucosa)
  • Transudative fluid accumulation (cervical/tubal fluid) prepares the environment for fertilization

🔬 Ciliated Cells vs. Peg (Secretory) Cells

Cell typeFunctionRegulation
Ciliated cellsBeat cilia toward the uterus → move the oocyte/embryo medially; create currents that carry fimbriae-swept oocyteEstrogen-dependent — most active at ovulation; cilia density peaks mid-cycle
Peg (non-ciliated / secretory) cellsSecrete tubal fluid (glycoproteins, nutrients) that nourishes the oocyte/early embryo, supports sperm capacitationEstrogen-dependent; peg cells appear "stripped" of organelles at apical surface
  • Both cell types are estrogen-responsive — estrogen drives cilia growth and secretory activity.
  • Progesterone (luteal phase) decreases ciliary activity and reduces estrogen-receptor expression.

💪 Muscularis

  • Two smooth-muscle layers:
    • Inner circular
    • Outer longitudinal
  • Peristaltic contractions work with ciliary action to propel the oocyte/embryo medially
  • The isthmus has the thickest muscularis — site of choice for tubal ligation

🛡️ Serosa

  • Outer layer of loose connective tissue + mesothelial surface (visceral peritoneum)
  • Continuous with the broad ligament (mesosalpinx) — so the oviducts are intra-peritoneal
⚠️ TRAP: "Tube Cells Make Ova" ▼
  • FALSE — ciliated and peg cells are somatic epithelial cells. They never produce oocytes. Oocytes are made in the ovary from fetal-era germ cells only.
  • Counterintuitive corollary: most tubal cancers are epithelial (not germ-cell) — high-grade serous carcinoma often originates in the fimbrial epithelium and spreads to the ovary. This is why risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO) in BRCA carriers removes both structures.

▼ 🔄 Function — Gamete Pickup, Fertilization, & Embryo Transit

⭐ The oviduct is the stage for three critical events: (1) fimbrial pickup of the oocyte, (2) fertilization in the ampulla, (3) cilia-driven transport of the early embryo to the uterus for implantation.

⏱️ Timeline from Ovulation to Implantation

Hour 0 (ovulation): fimbriae sweep over the ovarian surface; cilia create current that draws oocyte + corona radiata into the infundibulum
↓
Hour 0–24 (ampulla): if sperm are present (must have capacitated in female tract), fertilization occurs — sperm penetrates corona radiata → binds ZP3 → acrosome reaction → zona pellucida penetration → membrane fusion → cortical reaction (polyspermy block) → meiosis II completed → zygote formed
↓
Days 1–4 (cleavage): embryo undergoes cleavage divisions (2-cell → 4-cell → 8-cell → morula) while being propelled toward the uterus by cilia + peristalsis
↓
Day 4–5: morula enters the uterus, becomes blastocyst
↓
Day 6–7: implantation in decidualized endometrium

▼ 🚑 Clinical Correlates — Ectopic, Salpingitis, Hydrosalpinx

⭐ Tubal pathology is among the highest-yield topics on Step 1 — especially ectopic pregnancy and PID-related infertility. A damaged oviduct = impaired embryo transport = ectopic implantation risk.

🚨 Ectopic Pregnancy

  • Definition: implantation of an embryo outside the uterine cavity (~98% tubal; most commonly ampullary)
  • Presentation: amenorrhea + pelvic pain + vaginal bleeding; may present with rupture → hemodynamic instability + peritoneal signs
  • Labs: positive β-hCG but rising abnormally (failure to double every 48 h); lower than expected for gestational age
  • Imaging: transvaginal US shows empty uterus or "pseudogestational sac"; adnexal mass with "ring of fire" Doppler; possible free fluid (blood) in cul-de-sac
  • Risk factors: prior PID/salpingitis (most common), prior ectopic, prior tubal surgery, endometriosis, IUD use, smoking, ART/IVF
  • Management:
    • Stable + early (<3.5 cm, β-hCG < 5,000) → methotrexate (folate antagonist arrests trophoblast proliferation)
    • Unstable / ruptured → surgical salpingectomy
⚡ High-Yield Ectopic Facts:
  • Most common location = ampulla (70–80%).
  • Most dangerous (high rupture + hemorrhage) = interstitial / pars interstitialis.
  • Biggest modifiable risk factor = history of PID (Chlamydia/gonorrhea → scar/tubal dysfunction).
  • Always rule out in any reproductive-age woman with abdominal pain + β-hCG positive.

🦠 Salpingitis & PID

  • Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) = ascending infection of the uterus, oviducts, and/or ovaries; most often caused by Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
  • Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome — perihepatic inflammation ("violin-string adhesions") from ascending PID; classic RUQ pain mimicking cholecystitis.
  • Consequences: scarring of the tubal lining → impaired ciliary function → infertility, ectopic pregnancy, hydrosalpinx (tube distended by fluid), tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA).
  • Treatment: broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone + doxycycline ± metronidazole).

💧 Hydrosalpinx

  • Distal (fimbrial) blockage → fluid accumulates in the tube → "sausage-shaped" dilated tube on US/MRI
  • Classic sequela of chronic PID or prior tubal surgery
  • Severely reduces IVF success rates (inflammatory fluid leaks back into the uterus and impairs implantation) → salpingectomy is often recommended before IVF
⚠️ TRAP: Abdominal Pregnancy Is Not Tubal ▼
  • Abdominal pregnancy (~1% of ectopics) implants on peritoneal surfaces — bowel, omentum, pelvic sidewall. High mortality and hemorrhage risk.
  • Ovarian pregnancy (implantation on ovary) is also rare but possible.
  • These are NOT "tubal" ectopics but fall under the broader ectopic umbrella.

Testing Your Understanding — LO 2, Question 1

A 24-year-old woman with a history of two prior episodes of gonococcal cervicitis presents to the ED with acute unilateral pelvic pain and a positive urine β-hCG. Transvaginal ultrasound shows an empty uterus and a 2-cm adnexal mass with surrounding free fluid. Trace the pathophysiology from the original infection to the current presentation, and name the most likely tubal sub-site of the abnormal implantation.

Consider: PID → tubal scarring → impaired ciliary function; segments of the oviduct and their roles in transport; common ectopic sites

✓ Answer: Ampullary ectopic pregnancy from chronic salpingitis → impaired embryo transport.

Prior gonococcal/chlamydial cervicitis ascends to cause salpingitis → PID. Inflammation damages the ciliated epithelium and causes intraluminal scarring / adhesions, which impairs peristaltic and ciliary transport. A fertilized embryo moving from the ovary through the tube becomes "stuck" in the convoluted ampulla (the widest segment with the most mucosal folds — and by far the most common ectopic site, 70–80%). The embryo implants into thin tubal wall; β-hCG rises but abnormally; the mass grows until it either regresses, is surgically/medically treated, or ruptures with life-threatening hemorrhage. Management here (stable, β-hCG presumably low, early): methotrexate. Unstable or rupturing: salpingectomy. Consider: why would interstitial (pars interstitialis) ectopics be more catastrophic? (Thick myometrium disguises the growth; rupture causes massive hemorrhage from uterine vessels.)

▼ 🧠 Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Ovarian (HPO) Axis

⭐ The menstrual cycle is driven by a cascade: hypothalamus → pituitary → ovary → uterus. Every feedback loop in this system is tested on Step 1.

🧬 Cascade Overview

Hypothalamus: pulsatile GnRH every ~90 minutes
↓
Anterior pituitary: releases FSH (recruits follicles, aromatase in granulosa) + LH (androgen synthesis in theca; triggers ovulation; maintains corpus luteum)
↓
Ovary (follicle): makes estradiol (+ inhibin B) during follicular phase
↓
Ovary (corpus luteum): makes progesterone + estradiol + inhibin A during luteal phase
↓
Uterus / cervix / vagina: responds with proliferation, secretion, or shedding
⚡ HY — Feedback Rules:
  • Low estrogen → negative feedback on hypothalamus/pituitary (most of the cycle)
  • Sustained high estrogen (>200 pg/mL for ≥48 h) → POSITIVE feedback → LH surge → ovulation (the single most important exception to "estrogen is inhibitory")
  • Progesterone → always negative feedback; slows GnRH pulse frequency (luteal phase)
  • Inhibin B (granulosa, follicular phase) → selectively inhibits FSH
  • Inhibin A (corpus luteum, luteal phase) → also inhibits FSH
🧠 GnRH Pulsatility — Frequency Determines Output
▼
  • Fast pulses (~60 min) → favor LH (follicular phase end; LH surge).
  • Slow pulses (~180 min) → favor FSH (early follicular; progesterone slows pulses in luteal phase).
  • Continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH → desensitizes the pituitary → shuts down LH/FSH. This is the mechanism for GnRH agonists (leuprolide) in prostate cancer, endometriosis, precocious puberty.
⚠️ TRAP: "Leuprolide Stimulates Ovulation" ▼
  • A single dose of leuprolide (GnRH agonist) causes an initial flare (↑ LH/FSH). Continuous administration desensitizes pituitary GnRH receptors → suppression of LH/FSH → hypogonadism.
  • Used in: prostate cancer, endometriosis, central precocious puberty, uterine fibroids, IVF (to prevent premature LH surge).

▼ 📆 The Menstrual Cycle — Three Phases

⭐ A typical 28-day cycle has three endometrial phases (menses, proliferative, secretory) synchronized with two ovarian phases (follicular, luteal). By convention, day 1 = first day of menses.

🩸 Menses (Days 0–5)

  • Driven by progesterone withdrawal after corpus luteum regression
  • Spiral arteries constrict → ischemic necrosis of the functionalis → sheds as menstrual effluent (blood + tissue + endometrial glands)
  • Low estrogen and progesterone remove negative feedback on the hypothalamus → FSH/LH rise → new cohort of primordial follicles recruited
  • Average blood loss: ~30–40 mL; >80 mL = menorrhagia

🌱 Proliferative / Follicular Phase (Days 6–14)

  • Ovary: FSH recruits cohort → a dominant follicle emerges → secretes rising estradiol
  • Estrogen stimulates the endometrial basalis to regenerate a new functionalis:
    • Thickness doubles or triples (from ~1 mm post-menses to ~5 mm)
    • Glands are straight, tubular, non-secretory
    • Stromal cells proliferate; lots of mitoses
  • Cervical mucus becomes thin, watery, alkaline with "ferning" pattern — facilitates sperm transit
  • Vaginal epithelium: estrogen promotes glycogen deposition in superficial cells (fed to lactobacilli → lactic acid → low pH)
  • End of phase: sustained estrogen → LH surge → ovulation @ ~day 14
⚡ HY — Proliferative Features:
  • Histology: straight, thin, non-coiled glands; stroma is mitotically active.
  • Most variable phase — if the cycle is short or long, the variation is in the follicular phase. Luteal phase is constant at ~14 days.

🔄 Secretory / Luteal Phase (Days 15–28)

  • Ovary: post-ovulatory corpus luteum secretes progesterone + estradiol
  • Endometrium responds to progesterone:
    • Glands become coiled, convoluted, "saw-tooth" appearance
    • Glands secrete glycogen-rich nutritive fluid for a potential embryo
    • Spiral arteries lengthen and coil
    • Stromal decidualization begins (decidual reaction prepares for implantation)
  • Cervical mucus becomes thick and scanty (progesterone effect) → impedes further sperm entry
  • Basal body temperature rises ~0.5°F (progesterone thermogenic)
  • If no pregnancy: corpus luteum degenerates (no LH support) → progesterone drops → spiral arteries spasm → ischemic necrosis → menses starts

🤰 If Fertilization Occurs — hCG Rescue

  • Implantation (~day 21–22) → syncytiotrophoblast begins secreting β-hCG
  • hCG mimics LH → rescues the corpus luteum → continued progesterone production maintains the decidualized endometrium
  • Progesterone inhibits further ovulation (continuous negative feedback) and relaxes the myometrium (prevents contractions)
  • Around weeks 8–10, the placenta takes over progesterone production (luteal–placental shift)
  • Corpus luteum eventually regresses to corpus albicans by ~week 12
⚡ HY — Why the Luteal–Placental Shift Matters:
  • Before week 8: bilateral oophorectomy → loses progesterone → miscarriage.
  • After week 10: placenta fully supplies progesterone → oophorectomy is tolerated.
  • Progesterone supplementation ("17-OH progesterone caproate" or vaginal progesterone) is given in ART and in some patients with prior preterm birth.
⚠️ TRAP: "Ovulation is at Day 14 in Every Cycle" ▼
  • Ovulation is always ~14 days BEFORE the next menses — the luteal phase is fixed at ~14 days.
  • In a 32-day cycle, ovulation occurs around day 18, not day 14.
  • This matters for fertility counseling and natural family planning.

Testing Your Understanding — LO 3, Question 1

A 27-year-old patient undergoing IVF is given a GnRH agonist daily for 2 weeks prior to her egg-retrieval cycle to prevent a premature LH surge. Explain — at the receptor and cellular level — how a GnRH agonist paradoxically suppresses LH/FSH when endogenous GnRH stimulates it.

✓ Answer: Continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH receptor stimulation downregulates pituitary gonadotrophs → LH and FSH suppression.

Physiologic GnRH is pulsatile (~every 90 minutes); the gonadotroph's GnRH receptor cycles through binding, signaling, internalization, and resensitization. When a long-acting GnRH agonist (leuprolide, goserelin) provides constant receptor occupation, there is an initial flare (24–72 h of ↑ LH/FSH), followed by receptor down-regulation, internalization, and uncoupling from signaling pathways. ▼ 💧 Vestibular Glands — Bartholin & Skene

⭐ Two paired sets of exocrine glands flanking the vaginal vestibule produce lubricating secretions during arousal. Each has a male homologue and a classic clinical correlate.

🟡 Bartholin's Glands (Greater Vestibular Glands)

  • Location: 4 and 8 o'clock positions, posterolateral to the vaginal opening
  • Epithelium: simple columnar (mucus-secreting)
  • Secretion: mucus, discharged through a duct to the vestibule during arousal → lubrication
  • Male homologue: bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands
  • Clinical:
    • Bartholin cyst — duct obstruction → painless cystic swelling; incision & drainage or Word catheter if symptomatic

  • Location: alongside the distal female urethra
  • Epithelium: pseudostratified or simple columnar
  • Secretion: lubricating fluid (believed to be the source of "female ejaculate")
  • Male homologue: prostate gland
  • Clinical: Skene's duct cysts; source of some periurethral abscesses; adenocarcinoma is exceedingly rare

▼ 🌷 Mammary Glands — Structure & Clinical

⭐ The breast is a modified apocrine sweat gland. It is NOT a genital organ, but it is hormonally controlled and included here because it dramatically remodels during puberty, menstrual cycle, pregnancy, lactation, and menopause.

🧱 Structural Organization

  • 15–20 lobes of tubuloalveolar glands, separated by fibrous CT (Cooper's ligaments) and adipose tissue
  • Each lobe drains via a lactiferous duct through a dilation (lactiferous sinus) to the nipple
  • Each terminal duct lobular unit (TDLU) is the functional unit: terminal ductule + lobule (acini) — most breast cancers arise here
  • Ducts are lined by:
    • Inner luminal (epithelial) cells — secretory
    • Outer myoepithelial cells — contractile; eject milk during letdown; important in distinguishing benign from invasive cancer (invasive carcinoma lacks myoepithelial layer)

⚡ High-Yield Breast Facts:
  • Prolactin (anterior pituitary) stimulates milk synthesis. Dopamine inhibits prolactin — so dopamine antagonists (antipsychotics) cause galactorrhea.
  • Oxytocin (posterior pituitary) drives milk ejection via myoepithelial contraction ("let-down reflex"). Suckling → afferent signal → PVN/SON → oxytocin release.
  • Estrogen & progesterone from the placenta inhibit prolactin action on the breast during pregnancy; after delivery, placental hormones drop → prolactin unopposed → lactogenesis.
  • Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy in US women. Most arise from the TDLU (invasive ductal carcinoma is the most common histologic type).
  • BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations → markedly elevated risk of breast + ovarian cancer; manage with enhanced screening or risk-reducing mastectomy / salpingo-oophorectomy.

  • Invasive ductal carcinoma: cells breach the basement membrane; myoepithelial layer is absent.
  • Myoepithelial markers (p63, calponin, SMA) are the pathologist's go-to to distinguish these on biopsy.

A 54-year-old postmenopausal patient presents with recurrent vaginal itching, burning, and dyspareunia. On exam, the vaginal epithelium is thin, pale, and friable with petechiae. Vaginal pH is 6.5. In reproductive years, estrogen drives glycogen accumulation in superficial squamous cells of the vagina. Testing Your Understanding — LO 4, Question 2

A new mother is breastfeeding and experiences painful engorgement. When her infant begins to suckle, she feels a rush of warmth and her milk begins to flow. Name the two pituitary hormones involved, describe their specific actions on breast tissue, and explain the physiology of the let-down reflex.

Consider: anterior vs. posterior pituitary, alveolar secretion, myoepithelial contraction, neuroendocrine reflex

✓ Answer: Prolactin (anterior pituitary) drives milk synthesis; oxytocin (posterior pituitary) drives ejection via myoepithelial contraction.

Milk production requires two distinct pituitary hormones acting on different targets in the mammary gland: (1) Prolactin from the anterior pituitary acts on luminal secretory epithelium of the alveoli to synthesize milk proteins, lipids, and lactose. (2) Oxytocin from the posterior pituitary acts on myoepithelial cells wrapped around alveoli and ducts → contraction → milk ejection ("let-down"). The let-down reflex is a neuroendocrine arc: infant suckling → afferent signal via nerves → paraventricular & supraoptic nuclei → posterior pituitary → oxytocin release into blood. Oxytocin also causes postpartum uterine contractions (involution of the uterus and reduced bleeding). ✓ Correct: A — Luteal-placental shift occurs around weeks 8–10, with the placenta assuming progesterone production.

In the first ~8 weeks of pregnancy, the corpus luteum is the main source of progesterone, maintained by β-hCG (which mimics LH). Between weeks 8 and 10, the placenta takes over progesterone production — this is the luteal-placental shift. After this point, the corpus luteum (and thus the ovaries) becomes dispensable for pregnancy maintenance. In this case, at 10 weeks, oophorectomy is well-tolerated because the placenta is already the dominant source. B is incorrect — hCG acts on the ovary, not directly on the endometrium. C is wrong in the mature placental period. D — FSH has no role here; it's suppressed. E — fetal adrenal produces steroid precursors, not substantive progesterone at this stage.

LO 2 — Oviduct / Fallopian Tube (Q4–Q6)

Question 4

A 22-year-old woman presents to the ED with 6 hours of severe left lower quadrant pain, one episode of vomiting, and vaginal spotting. Urine β-hCG is positive at 3,200 mIU/mL. Transvaginal ultrasound shows no intrauterine gestation and a 2.5-cm complex mass in the left adnexa with surrounding free pelvic fluid. Which section of the oviduct is the most likely site of implantation?

A. Fimbriae

B. Infundibulum

C. Ampulla

D. Isthmus

E. Pars interstitialis

✓ Correct: C — Ampulla, the most common site of ectopic pregnancy (~70–80%).

The ampulla is the longest, widest section of the fallopian tube with highly folded mucosa — both the normal site of fertilization and by far the most common location for ectopic implantation. Scarring from prior PID, endometriosis, or tubal surgery impairs the ciliary and peristaltic transport → embryo gets stuck in the convoluted mucosa and implants. B (infundibulum) and A (fimbriae) are much less common. D (isthmus) is second-most common. E (pars interstitialis) is rare but the most dangerous due to catastrophic hemorrhage when it ruptures.

Question 5

A gynecologist performs a tubal ligation for permanent contraception by applying clips to the narrowest, most muscular segment of the fallopian tube just before it enters the uterus. Which segment is clipped?

A. Infundibulum

B. Ampulla

C. Isthmus

D. Pars interstitialis

E. Fimbriae

✓ Correct: C — Isthmus, the preferred site of tubal ligation.

The isthmus is the shortest and narrowest segment of the fallopian tube (just before it enters the uterus) with the thickest muscular wall. These features make it the ideal site for clips or rings in tubal ligation — occlusion is reliable, and the sturdy wall holds the ligation well. Ampullary occlusion is avoided because of its wider lumen and mucosal folds. Fimbrial surgery (salpingectomy of fimbriae) is another contraceptive option, especially given recent evidence that high-grade serous ovarian cancers often arise there.

Question 6

An investigator labels the two epithelial cell types of the fallopian tube. Estrogen administration causes cell type A to grow more apical cilia and cell type B to increase secretion of tubal fluid. Progesterone dampens both. Which statements about cell types A and B are correct?

A. Cell A is a ciliated cell; cell B is a peg (non-ciliated secretory) cell — both are estrogen-dependent

A. Cell A is a goblet cell; cell B is a ciliated cell — both are progesterone-dependent

C. Cell A is a ciliated cell; cell B is a paneth cell — both are independent of steroid hormones

D. Cell A is a neuroendocrine cell; cell B is a mucus cell — both are androgen-dependent

E. Cell A is a Sertoli cell; cell B is a Leydig cell

✓ Correct: A — Ciliated cells and peg (non-ciliated secretory) cells are the two epithelial cell types of the oviduct, both estrogen-dependent.

The simple columnar epithelium of the fallopian tube has exactly two cell types: (1) ciliated cells with apical cilia that beat toward the uterus to propel the oocyte/embryo; (2) peg cells (non-ciliated secretory) that secrete tubal fluid rich in glycoproteins and nutrients for the oocyte, sperm capacitation, and early embryo. Both are estrogen-responsive — cilia and secretion peak at ovulation. Progesterone (luteal phase) causes deciliation and reduced secretion. B (goblet) is intestinal/respiratory. C (paneth) is intestinal. D (neuroendocrine) is incorrect. E (Sertoli/Leydig) are testicular.

LO 3 — Uterus & Menstrual Cycle (Q7–Q10)

Question 7

A 25-year-old woman tracks her menstrual cycle and consistently has a 35-day cycle. She wants to know when she ovulates. Based on the physiology of the luteal phase, on approximately which day of her cycle does ovulation most likely occur?

A. Day 14

B. Day 17

C. Day 21

D. Day 28

E. Day 35

✓ Correct: C — Day 21 (35-day cycle minus 14-day luteal phase).

The luteal phase is essentially fixed at ~14 days — the corpus luteum's lifespan is programmed unless rescued by hCG. Cycle-length variation comes from the follicular phase. Therefore, ovulation = (cycle length) − 14. For a 35-day cycle: 35 − 14 = day 21. Day 14 (A) applies only to the canonical 28-day cycle. This rule is critical for fertility counseling and natural family planning — a common patient question.

Question 8

A 34-year-old woman undergoes an endometrial biopsy on cycle day 24. Histology shows elongated, tortuous glands with a "saw-toothed" appearance, coiled spiral arteries, and prominent stromal edema. Which of the following ovarian and hormonal states is consistent with these histologic findings?

A. Dominant follicle actively producing estradiol; progesterone low

B. Corpus luteum producing progesterone (and some estradiol)

C. Primordial follicle pool with low hormone production

D. Corpus albicans scar with no hormonal output

E. Atretic follicles with anovulation and absent hormone production

✓ Correct: B — Active corpus luteum secreting progesterone (secretory/luteal phase endometrium).

The histologic features described — coiled "saw-tooth" glands, spiral arteries, and stromal edema/decidualization — are pathognomonic of the secretory (luteal) phase, driven by progesterone from the corpus luteum. Day 24 is firmly in the luteal phase of a 28-day cycle. A (proliferative) would show straight tubular glands and mitotically active stroma. C, D, and E all describe low-hormone states that do not produce the secretory transformation.

Question 9

A 45-year-old woman with uterine fibroids is started on monthly leuprolide injections to shrink the fibroids before surgery. Three days after her first dose, she reports worsening pelvic pain and heavy bleeding. By week 2, her bleeding has stopped. Which mechanism explains the transient worsening followed by suppression?

A. Initial GnRH-receptor flare followed by receptor downregulation and gonadotropin suppression

B. Direct toxicity of leuprolide on uterine smooth muscle

C. Competitive antagonism of the androgen receptor

D. Inhibition of aromatase leading to estrogen depletion

E. Blockade of LH receptors on the corpus luteum

✓ Correct: A — Leuprolide is a GnRH agonist: initial "flare" (↑ LH/FSH) followed by receptor desensitization and gonadotropin suppression.

Physiologic GnRH is pulsatile; constant receptor stimulation causes internalization and uncoupling of gonadotroph GnRH receptors. For the first 1–2 weeks of leuprolide therapy, there is a paradoxical flare (↑ LH/FSH, ↑ estradiol) before receptor desensitization suppresses gonadotropin release and creates a hypoestrogenic state. The hypoestrogenic environment shrinks estrogen-dependent fibroids. Clinical pearl: to prevent the flare in prostate cancer treatment, patients are co-treated with an androgen-receptor antagonist (flutamide, bicalutamide) for the first month.

Question 10

A 30-year-old woman has had amenorrhea for 6 months following a D&C for retained products of conception 1 year ago. Her serum FSH, LH, and estradiol are normal. After a progestin challenge (oral medroxyprogesterone acetate), she does not have withdrawal bleeding. Hysterosalpingography shows filling defects consistent with intrauterine adhesions. Which endometrial layer is most directly affected?

A. Myometrium

B. Perimetrium

C. Functionalis of the endometrium

D. Basalis of the endometrium

E. Cervical transformation zone

✓ Correct: D — Damage to the basalis (Asherman syndrome).

The basalis is the deep, non-shedding endometrial layer supplied by straight arteries. It regenerates the functionalis after every menses. Aggressive instrumentation (D&C, especially postpartum for retained POC) or severe endometritis can strip or scar the basalis → intrauterine adhesions (Asherman syndrome). Without a functional basalis, no new functionalis is formed — so progestin withdrawal cannot induce bleeding despite normal hormones. Treatment: hysteroscopic lysis of adhesions + estrogen therapy to promote basalis regrowth.

LO 4 — Cervix, Vagina, External Genitalia & Breast (Q11–Q14)

Question 11

A 38-year-old woman has an abnormal Pap smear showing high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL). Colposcopy with biopsy of the transformation zone confirms CIN III with extensive koilocytic change. PCR identifies HPV type 16. Which viral proteins drive the malignant transformation, and what cellular pathways do they disable?

A. E1 and E2 — inactivate telomerase

B. E6 and E7 — E6 degrades p53 and E7 inactivates Rb

C. L1 and L2 — capsid proteins that drive replication

D. Tax protein — activates NF-κB

E. HBx — disrupts cell cycle via p21

✓ Correct: B — HPV E6 degrades p53; E7 inactivates Rb → loss of cell-cycle checkpoints.

High-risk HPV types (16, 18, 31, 33, 45, etc.) produce two oncoproteins that disable core tumor-suppressor pathways: E6 promotes ubiquitin-mediated degradation of p53 (the G1/S DNA-damage checkpoint) and E7 binds and inactivates Rb (releasing E2F → unopposed cell-cycle progression). Together they produce uncontrolled proliferation in the transformation zone where immature squamous metaplastic cells are most susceptible. This drives dysplasia → CIN → invasive cervical cancer. L1/L2 are capsid proteins (basis for Gardasil vaccine). Tax is from HTLV-1 (ATLL). HBx is from HBV (hepatocellular carcinoma).

Question 12

An 18-year-old woman presents with a painful, tender swelling at the 4 o'clock position posterolateral to the vaginal introitus. There is overlying erythema and induration. Gram stain of expressed fluid grows gram-negative diplococci. The abscess is located in a gland that is the female homologue of which male structure?

A. Prostate

B. Bulbourethral (Cowper's) gland

C. Seminal vesicle

D. Epididymis

E. Glans penis

✓ Correct: B — Bartholin's gland abscess; Bartholin's is the homologue of the male bulbourethral (Cowper's) gland.

Bartholin's glands sit at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions posterolateral to the vaginal introitus and drain via a duct into the vestibule. An abscess typically presents as a painful, tender, fluctuant mass with erythema. Causative organisms include Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis, and mixed aerobic/anaerobic flora. Treatment is incision and drainage (often with Word catheter) plus antibiotics. Male homologue: bulbourethral (Cowper's) gland — both arise from the urogenital sinus and produce lubricating mucus during arousal. Prostate (A) is the male homologue of Skene's glands, NOT Bartholin's.

Question 13

A 25-year-old woman in her third day postpartum begins breastfeeding her newborn. When her infant latches and begins suckling, she experiences uterine cramping and a rush of milk from both breasts. Which hormone is primarily responsible for the milk ejection and uterine cramping, and where is it released?

A. Prolactin — anterior pituitary

B. Oxytocin — posterior pituitary

C. FSH — anterior pituitary

D. Estradiol — ovaries

E. Dopamine — hypothalamus

✓ Correct: B — Oxytocin from the posterior pituitary.

Suckling triggers the let-down reflex: sensory afferents from the nipple → hypothalamic paraventricular (PVN) and supraoptic (SON) nuclei → oxytocin release from the posterior pituitary. Oxytocin acts on myoepithelial cells around breast alveoli → milk ejection, and on uterine smooth muscle → contraction (involution of the uterus post-partum, decreased bleeding, and the characteristic breastfeeding cramps). Prolactin (A) — made in response to the same suckling cue, but its role is milk synthesis, not ejection. Dopamine (E) inhibits prolactin release; FSH (C) and estradiol (D) are not involved in lactation mechanics.

Question 14

A pathologist examines a needle biopsy from a suspicious breast mass. Low-power microscopy shows malignant epithelial cells confined within a duct-like space; surrounding the cells are spindle-shaped cells that stain positive for p63 and smooth muscle actin. High-power views show no evidence of basement membrane invasion. Which best classifies this lesion?

A. Invasive ductal carcinoma

B. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)

C. Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS)

D. Fibroadenoma

E. Paget disease of the breast

✓ Correct: B — Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS): malignant cells within the duct, intact myoepithelial layer, no invasion through basement membrane.

The key histologic feature distinguishing in-situ from invasive carcinoma is the preserved myoepithelial layer (positive for p63, SMA, calponin) surrounding the neoplastic duct. DCIS is confined to the ducts and has not breached the basement membrane — it is a non-obligate precursor to invasive ductal carcinoma. A would lack the myoepithelial layer. C (LCIS) would show loss of E-cadherin and non-cohesive cells in lobules. D (fibroadenoma) is a benign biphasic tumor with stromal proliferation. E (Paget disease) shows intraepithelial malignant cells in the nipple skin, often with underlying DCIS or invasive cancer. Management of DCIS: wide local excision ± radiation; some estrogen-receptor-positive cases also get endocrine therapy.
