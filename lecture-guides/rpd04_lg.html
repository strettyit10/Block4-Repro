▼ ⚽ Testicular Descent & Cryptorchidism

⭐ The testes originate near the kidneys (same intermediate mesoderm origin) and descend into the scrotum by month 7 of gestation, guided by the gubernaculum. This moves them to a cooler environment (2–4°C below core) required for normal spermatogenesis.

🧭 Timeline & Mechanism

~Week 13: testes begin developing in the abdomen, near the embryonic kidneys
↓
Months 2–7: descent down the posterior abdominal wall → through inguinal canal → into scrotum
↓
Month 7: fetal androgens (testosterone/DHT) trigger gubernacular contraction → pulls testes into scrotum
↓
After descent: processus vaginalis should obliterate (leaving tunica vaginalis around testis)
⚡ Why Descent Matters:
  • Scrotal temperature = ~34°C (core is 37°C).
  • Spermatogenesis requires this cooler environment — high heat damages Sertoli cells + germ cells (Leydig cells are relatively heat-resistant).
  • Embryology crossover: the mesonephric tubules → efferent ducts, and the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct → epididymis + vas deferens + seminal vesicles. Testes + their plumbing share kidney embryology.

🎯 Cryptorchidism — Undescended Testis

  • Definition: one or both testes fail to descend into the scrotum (usually stuck in inguinal canal)
  • Most common congenital GU anomaly in males — ~3% of term, ~30% of preterm
  • Consequences if uncorrected:
    • ↓ Fertility — heat damages seminiferous tubules, impairs spermatogenesis
    • ↑ Risk of germ cell tumors (classically seminoma) — risk persists even after orchidopexy
    • Indirect inguinal hernia (persistent processus vaginalis), testicular torsion
  • Labs:
    • Testosterone: normal (Leydig cells heat-resistant)
    • Inhibin B: ↓ (Sertoli dysfunction from heat)
    • FSH: ↑ (loss of inhibin B feedback)
  • Management: orchidopexy (surgical fixation) before age 1 — earlier intervention preserves more fertility & decreases (but does not eliminate) malignancy risk
⚠️ TRAP: Testosterone Levels in Cryptorchidism ▼
  • Testosterone is usually normal — Leydig cells are heat-resistant. What's affected is spermatogenesis: inhibin B falls, FSH rises.
  • Classic Step 1 stem: "40 yo man with history of undescended testis presents with infertility. Labs show ↑ FSH, normal testosterone, ↓ inhibin B."

▼ 🌡️ Pampiniform Plexus & Testicular Torsion

⭐ The pampiniform plexus is a web of veins that wraps around the testicular artery. It acts as a countercurrent heat exchanger — cooling warm arterial blood before it reaches the testis (maintaining scrotal temperature for spermatogenesis).

🧬 Structure & Function

  • A dense venous plexus surrounding the testicular artery within the spermatic cord
  • Warm blood descending in the artery transfers heat to cooler blood ascending in the veins → countercurrent heat exchange
  • Drains to the right testicular vein → IVC (at acute angle), left testicular vein → left renal vein (at 90° angle)

🔵 Varicocele

  • Dilated pampiniform plexus, classically described as a "bag of worms" on exam
  • Left side predominant (~90%) — because the left testicular vein enters the left renal vein at a right angle, increasing backpressure
  • Clinical red flag: a right-sided varicocele OR a new left varicocele that does NOT decompress when supine → evaluate for renal cell carcinoma (invading the renal vein / IVC)
  • Long-standing varicocele → impaired thermoregulation → infertility (heat damages spermatogenesis)

🔄 Testicular Torsion

  • Twisting of the spermatic cord → occludes venous then arterial blood flow → ischemic infarction of testis
  • Most common in adolescents (12–18 years); "bell-clapper deformity" (abnormally free testis within tunica vaginalis) is a predisposing anatomic variant
  • Presentation: sudden, severe unilateral testicular pain; testis may appear high-riding and transverse; loss of cremasteric reflex; no relief with elevation (negative Prehn sign — vs. epididymitis, where elevation relieves pain)
  • Imaging: Doppler US shows ↓/absent flow
  • Management: surgical emergency — detorsion within 6 hours to preserve the testis; bilateral orchiopexy to prevent recurrence
⚠️ TRAP: Torsion vs. Epididymitis (Prehn Sign) ▼
  • Torsion — sudden onset, no relief with scrotal elevation (negative Prehn), absent cremasteric reflex, absent Doppler flow → surgical detorsion NOW.
  • Epididymitis — gradual onset, pain relieved by elevation (positive Prehn), cremasteric reflex preserved, increased Doppler flow → antibiotics (GC/Chlamydia in young; coliforms in older men).

▼ 🧬 Leydig & Sertoli Cells — Two Cell Types, Two Functions

⭐ The testis has two functional cell types: Leydig cells (between tubules, make testosterone) and Sertoli cells (inside seminiferous tubules, support germ cells). Germ cells are the third "cargo" — not endocrine or support.

🔶 Leydig Cells — The Endocrine Cells

  • Location: interstitial — between seminiferous tubules, in the loose CT
  • Function: steroidogenesis — synthesize testosterone from cholesterol (via same pathway as ovarian theca)
  • Regulation: LH from anterior pituitary binds Leydig cell → cAMP-mediated testosterone synthesis
  • Histology:
    • Abundant lipid droplets (cholesterol storage)
    • Prominent smooth ER (steroid synthesis)
    • Mitochondria with tubular cristae (steroid hormone signature)
    • Characteristic Reinke crystals (rod-shaped cytoplasmic inclusions) — found only in Leydig cells
  • Testosterone effects:
    • Supports spermatogenesis (paracrine — locally diffuses into seminiferous tubules)
    • Maintains accessory glands (prostate, seminal vesicles, bulbourethral)
    • Male secondary sex characteristics; libido
    • Converted to more potent DHT (50× more potent) by 5α-reductase in target tissues
⚡ Reinke Crystals — A Key Histologic Buzzword:
  • Rectangular, eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusions unique to Leydig cells.
  • Often asked on path questions as an identifier for Leydig cell tumors (which may cause precocious puberty in boys or gynecomastia via peripheral testosterone → estradiol conversion).

🔷 Sertoli Cells — The Support Cells

  • Location: inside seminiferous tubules, attached to the basal lamina and extending to the lumen
  • Function:
    • Nurse cells — nourish and physically support developing germ cells
    • Phagocytose residual bodies (excess cytoplasm shed during spermiogenesis)
    • Secrete androgen-binding protein (ABP) → concentrates testosterone within seminiferous tubules (paracrine, 100× higher local concentration)
    • Secrete inhibin B → negative feedback on FSH
    • Secrete Müllerian Inhibiting Substance (MIS/AMH) during fetal life → regresses paramesonephric ducts
    • Form the blood-testis barrier (see below)
    • Contain aromatase → local conversion of some testosterone to estradiol
  • Regulation: FSH (primary); also testosterone from neighboring Leydig cells (paracrine)
  • Histology: tall, columnar cells spanning the seminiferous epithelium from basal lamina to lumen; irregular outlines from enveloping germ cells

🔒 The Blood-Testis Barrier

Adjacent Sertoli cells form tight junctions (Sertoli-Sertoli junctions), creating a barrier that divides the seminiferous tubule into two compartments:

  • Basal compartment (below the junctions) — contains spermatogonia; open to systemic circulation; mitotic activity (spermatogonia divide here)
  • Adluminal compartment (above the junctions) — contains primary spermatocytes → spermatids → spermatozoa; meiotic and post-meiotic cells
Why the barrier matters:
1. Meiotic + haploid germ cells express new surface antigens not present at fetal immune tolerance
→ Immune system would view them as foreign
2. Barrier sequesters these cells from systemic circulation — prevents autoimmune orchitis
3. Creates a unique microenvironment (high testosterone, specialized nutrients) maintained by Sertoli cells
⚡ Clinical Tie-Ins:
  • Vasectomy reversal / testicular trauma → anti-sperm antibodies can form when sperm antigens leak → infertility.
  • Mumps orchitis disrupts the barrier → post-mumps infertility.
  • Sertoli-cell-only syndrome (Del Castillo): seminiferous tubules contain ONLY Sertoli cells (no germ cells) → azoospermia, high FSH, normal testosterone/LH.
  • Androgen-binding protein (ABP) keeps intratubular testosterone at ~100× plasma level — needed for spermatogenesis.
🧠 Sertoli Cell Functions — "F SHIPS MAnufacture"
▼

Sertoli cells do a lot. Key secretions:

  • Inhibin B (suppresses FSH)
  • ABP (androgen-binding protein — concentrates testosterone)
  • MIS / AMH (fetal Müllerian regression)
  • Form blood-testis barrier via tight junctions
  • Respond to FSH + testosterone
  • Convert T → estradiol via aromatase
⚠️ TRAP: Androgen Insensitivity vs. 5α-Reductase Deficiency ▼
  • Complete Androgen Insensitivity (CAIS): Androgen receptor nonfunctional → 46,XY with female external phenotype. Leydig still make testosterone (↑), MIS works → no uterus.
  • 5α-reductase deficiency: Testosterone can't be converted to DHT → ambiguous/female external genitalia at birth, but virilization at puberty. Internal ducts normal (Wolffian structures depend on T, not DHT).
  • DHT is 50× more potent than testosterone at the androgen receptor; it drives external virilization and prostate development.

▼ 🧠 Hypothalamus-Pituitary-Gonadal (HPG) Axis

⭐ The HPG axis is the master regulator of spermatogenesis and testosterone production. Two pituitary hormones (LH, FSH) target two testicular cell types (Leydig, Sertoli) with two distinct feedback loops.

⚡ HY — Predicting Hormone Patterns:
  • Primary testicular failure (Klinefelter, post-chemo, cryptorchidism): ↓ T, ↓ inhibin B → ↑↑ LH, ↑↑ FSH (hypergonadotropic).
  • Hypothalamic / pituitary failure (Kallmann, pituitary adenoma): ↓ GnRH → ↓ LH, ↓ FSH, ↓ T (hypogonadotropic).
  • Anabolic steroid use: exogenous T suppresses LH → ↓ endogenous T → testicular atrophy + infertility (often with elevated estradiol from aromatization → gynecomastia).
  • Sertoli-cell-only syndrome: azoospermia with isolated ↑ FSH, normal LH, normal T (Leydig cells intact).

⚡ HY Spermatogenesis Facts:
  • ~74 days from spermatogonium to mature spermatozoon in the seminiferous tubule.
  • Additional ~12–21 days for maturation in the epididymis.

▼ 🏁 Spermiogenesis & Mature Sperm Anatomy

⭐ Spermiogenesis is the final remodeling step — a round spermatid (n) becomes a streamlined motile spermatozoon. No further division; just morphologic transformation.

🧬 Key Events of Spermiogenesis

  1. Acrosome formation — Golgi-derived cap over the nucleus, containing hydrolytic enzymes (hyaluronidase, acrosin) to penetrate corona radiata & zona pellucida during fertilization
  2. Nuclear condensation — histones replaced by protamines → compact nucleus
  3. Flagellum formation — centriole-derived axoneme (9+2 microtubule arrangement) grows from the tail of the cell
  4. Mitochondrial sheath — mitochondria coil around the axoneme in the midpiece (ATP for motility)
  5. Cytoplasm elimination — excess cytoplasm shed as "residual bodies" phagocytosed by Sertoli cells

⚡ Kartagener Syndrome (Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia):
  • Dynein arm defect → immotile cilia AND immotile sperm flagella (axoneme structure is identical).
  • Triad: situs inversus, chronic sinusitis & bronchiectasis, infertility.
  • Perfect Step 1 "connect the dots" question — 9+2 axoneme motility defect hits sperm tail + respiratory cilia + embryonic node ciliated cells.
⚠️ TRAP: Spermatogenesis vs. Spermiogenesis ▼
  • Spermatogenesis = the entire process, spermatogonium → spermatozoon, including mitosis + meiosis + remodeling.
  • Spermiogenesis = the remodeling step only (spermatid → spermatozoon), post-meiotic, no further division.
  • Every spermiogenesis event happens inside spermatogenesis, but not every spermatogenesis event is spermiogenesis.

🧠 Testing Your Understanding!

Testing Your Understanding — LO 1, Question 1

A 28-year-old man with a history of unilateral cryptorchidism treated with orchidopexy at age 8 presents with infertility. Semen analysis shows azoospermia. Labs: testosterone 420 ng/dL (normal), LH 9 mIU/mL (normal-high), FSH 28 mIU/mL (high), inhibin B 20 pg/mL (low). Explain the hormonal pattern in terms of Leydig vs. Sertoli cell heat sensitivity.

Consider: heat-resistant Leydig cells, heat-sensitive Sertoli/germ cells, negative feedback loops, inhibin B

✓ Answer: Selective Sertoli/germ cell damage from prolonged heat exposure — Leydig function preserved.

The scrotal position of the testis normally keeps the testes ~2–4°C cooler than core body temperature. With cryptorchidism, the abdominal environment damages heat-sensitive seminiferous tubules — specifically the Sertoli cells and germ cells. Leydig cells are heat-resistant, so testosterone production continues normally (normal T, normal-high LH). The Sertoli-cell damage reduces inhibin B, which selectively inhibits FSH → loss of this feedback causes isolated FSH elevation. Spermatogenesis is severely impaired → azoospermia. The late orchidopexy (age 8) did not prevent the damage — intervention before age 1 preserves much more fertility and modestly decreases malignancy risk. Consider: what additional surveillance is warranted long-term? (↑ risk of seminoma — regular self-exam + clinical follow-up.)

Testing Your Understanding — LO 1, Question 2

A researcher uses electron microscopy to examine cells within the interstitium of the testis and identifies rectangular cytoplasmic inclusions, abundant smooth endoplasmic reticulum, and mitochondria with tubular cristae. Name the cell type, its principal hormone, and the transcriptional regulation that drives its secretion.

Consider: Reinke crystals, cholesterol side-chain cleavage, LH receptor pathway

✓ Answer: Leydig cells producing testosterone, stimulated by LH via the cAMP-PKA pathway.

The three ultrastructural features are the classic tip-off for Leydig cells: (1) Reinke crystals — rectangular eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions unique to Leydig cells; (2) abundant smooth ER reflecting active steroid synthesis (membranes for enzymes); (3) tubular cristae in mitochondria — a morphologic signature of steroid-producing cells (also seen in adrenal cortex). LH binds its GPCR on Leydig cells → Gs → adenylyl cyclase → ↑ cAMP → PKA activation → phosphorylates StAR (steroidogenic acute regulatory protein), which moves cholesterol into the mitochondrion → CYP11A1 (side-chain cleavage) converts cholesterol to pregnenolone → subsequent enzymes generate testosterone. Consider: how would the histology differ in prolonged chronic LH stimulation (e.g., Klinefelter)? (Leydig cell hyperplasia — fewer tubules, more interstitial Leydig cells dominating the image.)

▼ 🗺️ Overview — Sperm's Exit Pathway

⭐ Sperm produced in the seminiferous tubules are immotile and dilute. They must travel through a carefully-staged system of ducts to concentrate, mature, gain motility, and finally be propelled during ejaculation.

📍 Master Pathway

Seminiferous tubule (production, Sertoli+germ cells)
↓
Tubulus rectus (straight tubules — exit door)
↓
Rete testis (network in mediastinum testis)
↓
Efferent ductules (~20 ducts; reabsorb fluid → concentrate sperm)
↓

↓

↓

SEVE(N) UP — sperm's path from production to penis:

  • Seminiferous tubules

  • Vas deferens
  • Ejaculatory duct
  • (Nothing / "Nothing")
  • Urethra

  • Between S and E: tubulus rectus → rete testis → efferent ductules round out the full pathway.

▼ ➡️ Tubulus Rectus & Rete Testis — Intratesticular Start

⭐ The first two segments are intratesticular — they live inside the testis and simply collect sperm-containing fluid from all the seminiferous tubules into a single route toward the efferent ductules.

🔹 Tubulus Rectus (Straight Tubules)

  • Structure: short, straight transitional tubules
  • Epithelium: simple cuboidal (lacking Sertoli cells — cleanly Sertoli-cell-only then transitions to cuboidal)
  • Function: direct exit from seminiferous tubule into rete testis

🔹 Rete Testis

  • Structure: anastomosing network of small channels embedded in the mediastinum testis (posterior testicular connective tissue)
  • Epithelium: simple cuboidal with occasional microvilli and cilia to help move fluid/sperm
  • Surrounded by: dense connective tissue with blood and lymphatic vessels
  • Function: collects output from all seminiferous tubules and funnels it to efferent ductules

▼ 🌊 Efferent Ductules — The Concentration Step

⭐ The efferent ductules (~20 small ducts) connect the rete testis to the head of the epididymis. Their defining job is to reabsorb most of the testicular fluid, dramatically concentrating sperm before they enter the epididymis.

🧬 Structure & Embryology

  • Origin: derived from mesonephric tubules — that's why they retain water/solute-reabsorbing capability similar to renal tubules
  • Characteristic histologic appearance: "scalloped" or "toothed" luminal border — alternating short non-ciliated (resorptive) cells and tall ciliated (motile) cells
  • Epithelium:
    • Tall ciliated cells — beat to move sperm toward epididymis
    • Short non-ciliated resorptive cells — reabsorb ~90% of testicular fluid
⚡ HY Distinguishing Feature:
  • The "scalloped luminal border" from alternating tall + short cells is the #1 Step 1 buzzphrase for efferent ductules.
  • Contrast with the smooth, straight "brush border" of epididymis (stereocilia).

▼ 🌀 Epididymis — Maturation & Storage

⭐ The epididymis is a single, ~7-meter highly coiled duct sitting on the posterior testis. Sperm mature (gain motility + fertilizing ability) and are stored here. It has three gross regions.

📘 Histology

  • Epithelium: pseudostratified columnar with stereocilia (long, non-motile microvilli — NOT true cilia)
    • Principal cells absorb fluid and secrete factors that mature sperm
    • Basal cells sit on the basement membrane (progenitor cells)
  • Muscular layer: thin smooth muscle — contractions increase during ejaculation
  • Stereocilia are a key Step 1 finding — they are actually long microvilli, not cilia (no motility, no 9+2 axoneme).

📍 Three Regions (Head, Body, Tail)

RegionFunctionSperm state
Head (caput)Receives efferent ductules; initial fluid absorptionSperm immature — cannot fertilize yet
Body (corpus)Progressive maturation; absorption + secretionSperm gaining motility
Tail (cauda)Wider lumen slows fluid → storageSperm fully mature; can fertilize; storage up to ~1 month
⚡ HY Epididymis Facts:
  • Epididymis is the classic site of epididymitis — in young men, think Chlamydia trachomatis + Neisseria gonorrhoeae; in older men, think E. coli, Pseudomonas, or other coliforms.
  • Prehn sign: elevation of scrotum relieves epididymitis pain but NOT torsion pain.
  • Sperm develop motility + fertilizing capacity during transit — this is a passive maturation driven by epididymal secretions, not by sperm metabolism alone.
⚠️ TRAP: Stereocilia vs. True Cilia ▼
  • Stereocilia (epididymis, vas deferens, hair cells of inner ear) = long microvilli. Actin-based, NON-motile, no 9+2 axoneme.
  • True cilia (respiratory tract, oviduct, efferent ductules) = 9+2 microtubule arrangement, motile via dynein.
  • Step 1 favorite: "long apical projections without 9+2 axoneme" = stereocilia, not cilia.

▼ 🚀 Vas Deferens & Vasectomy

⭐ The vas (ductus) deferens is a thick, muscular tube that carries sperm from the tail of the epididymis toward the ejaculatory duct. Its defining feature is a prominent three-layer smooth-muscle wall that propels sperm by peristalsis during ejaculation.

🔬 Histology

  • Epithelium: pseudostratified columnar with stereocilia (similar to epididymis, but often shorter stereocilia)
  • Lumen: narrow and relatively irregular
  • Muscularis: THREE layers of smooth muscle:
    • Inner longitudinal
    • Middle circular (thickest)
    • Outer longitudinal
  • Innervation: sympathetic (hypogastric plexus) → peristaltic contraction during emission phase of ejaculation

✂️ Vasectomy

  • A permanent male contraception procedure: the vas deferens is cut and a segment removed, or tied off with sutures
  • Effect: sperm cannot reach the ejaculate; testosterone production and ejaculate volume are unaffected (seminal/prostatic fluid continues)
  • Sperm continue to be produced by testis → reabsorbed in the epididymis/vas stump
  • Post-procedure: takes 2–3 months / ~20 ejaculations for residual sperm downstream of the ligation to be cleared; backup contraception required until semen analysis confirms azoospermia
  • Potential complications: sperm granulomas, anti-sperm antibodies (from leaked sperm antigens crossing damaged blood-testis barrier)
⚠️ TRAP: Thick Muscular Wall ▼
  • On histology, the vas deferens is immediately recognizable by its disproportionately thick three-layer smooth-muscle wall relative to its small lumen. This is the #1 distinguisher vs. epididymis (which has a thin muscular coat).
  • Classic image: tiny stellate lumen surrounded by a massive muscularis.

▼ 🔀 Ampulla & Ejaculatory Duct

⭐ The ampulla of the vas deferens is the dilated distal end, located behind the bladder. It joins the duct of the seminal vesicle to form the ejaculatory duct, which traverses the prostate to empty into the prostatic urethra.

📘 Structure & Function

  • Ampulla: widened, folded terminal segment of the vas; mixing site where sperm from the epididymis meets secretions from accessory glands
  • Ejaculatory duct: short (~2 cm), formed by union of ampulla + seminal vesicle duct; passes through the prostate and opens on the verumontanum (seminal colliculus) of the prostatic urethra
  • Epithelium: simple/pseudostratified columnar; surrounded by less muscle than the main vas

Testing Your Understanding — LO 2, Question 1

A pathology resident is shown a high-magnification image of a male reproductive duct. The epithelium is characterized by alternating tall ciliated cells and short non-ciliated cells, producing a "scalloped" or "toothed" luminal profile. Name the structure, its embryologic origin, and its primary physiologic role in sperm handling.

Consider: mesonephric tubules, fluid reabsorption, ciliary transport, concentration of sperm

✓ Answer: Efferent ductules — mesonephric tubule origin; their role is to reabsorb testicular fluid and concentrate sperm.

The "scalloped" luminal profile is the single-best visual cue for the efferent ductules. These ~20 small ducts connect the rete testis to the head of the epididymis. Their key histologic feature is the alternation of tall ciliated cells (transport) and short non-ciliated resorptive cells (absorption), which creates the toothed/saw-tooth appearance. Embryologically, they derive from mesonephric tubules — the same tissue that forms kidney tubules, which is why they retain strong fluid-resorptive capacity. Functionally: testicular fluid arriving from the rete testis is ~90% reabsorbed here, concentrating sperm dramatically before they enter the epididymis for maturation and storage. Consider: what would happen with a selective efferent-ductule transport defect? (Accumulated fluid → testicular fluid backup → obstructive oligo/azoospermia.)

Testing Your Understanding — LO 2, Question 2

A 32-year-old man requests permanent contraception via vasectomy. After the procedure, he asks why he still needs backup contraception for the next 3 months and why his testosterone and ejaculate volume are expected to remain unchanged. Explain the anatomy and physiology that justify both points.

Consider: sperm storage distal to the vasectomy site, fluid contributions to ejaculate, Leydig cell testosterone production

✓ Answer: Residual sperm downstream of the ligation must be cleared; ejaculate volume and testosterone are dominated by accessory gland and Leydig-cell output, both unaffected by vasectomy.

Vasectomy cuts/ties the vas deferens, blocking sperm transit from the epididymis to the ejaculatory duct. However, downstream of the ligation site — in the ampulla, seminal vesicles, and ejaculatory duct — there are residual mature sperm. These take ~2–3 months (or ~20 ejaculations) to be cleared. Semen analysis is required to confirm azoospermia before discontinuing backup contraception. As for unchanged ejaculate/testosterone: (1) ~70% of ejaculate volume is from seminal vesicles (fructose-rich fluid), ~25% from prostate (PSA, zinc, acid phosphatase), and only a small percentage from the epididymis/vas. None of those glands are affected by vasectomy. (2) Testosterone is made by Leydig cells in the testicular interstitium — independent of ductal plumbing. Leydig function is preserved, as is LH/FSH feedback. Consider: what might cause infertility if the patient later tries to reverse the vasectomy? (Anti-sperm antibodies — leakage of sperm antigens post-vasectomy can cross the damaged blood-testis barrier, sensitizing the immune system.)

▼ 🍯 Seminal Vesicles

⭐ Paired accessory glands sitting posterior to the bladder. Each is a coiled tube emptying into the vas deferens to form the ejaculatory duct.

🔬 Histology

  • Epithelium: pseudostratified or simple columnar
  • Mucosa is HIGHLY folded with complex labyrinthine infoldings → massive surface area for secretion; classic "honeycomb" histologic appearance
  • Secretory cells have lipofuscin pigment (golden-brown granules) accumulating with age
  • Smooth muscle coat contracts during ejaculation to empty contents

🧪 Secretion

  • Alkaline, viscous fluid rich in:
    • Fructose — primary sperm energy source (hexokinase in sperm head uses it for ATP)
    • Prostaglandins — stimulate smooth-muscle contractility in female genital tract
    • Semenogelin — clotting factor that forms the initial semen coagulum; later cleaved by PSA to liquefy
    • Vitamin C, flavins, potassium
  • NOT a sperm storage site — sperm transit through but are not stored here
  • Testosterone-dependent: androgen deprivation causes atrophy
⚡ HY Clinical Tie-In:
  • Absent or low fructose in semen → suggests ejaculatory duct obstruction or congenital absence of seminal vesicles.
  • Congenital Bilateral Absence of the Vas Deferens (CBAVD) is associated with CFTR mutations (cystic fibrosis spectrum) — these patients also often lack seminal vesicles → low ejaculate volume, low fructose, obstructive azoospermia.

▼ 🏛️ Prostate Gland

⭐ The prostate is a walnut-sized gland encircling the prostatic urethra just below the bladder. It has four distinct zones with different pathologies, and its secretion contains the clinically-used biomarker PSA.

🔬 Histology

  • Epithelium: simple or pseudostratified columnar lining glandular alveoli
  • Corpora amylacea: small, round, eosinophilic, concentrically laminated calcified bodies in the prostatic lumina — classic histologic signature; increase with age
  • Verumontanum (seminal colliculus) — midline prominence of the posterior prostatic urethra where ejaculatory ducts open; also the boundary between bladder-cancer and urothelial histology considerations
  • Two-cell epithelium: luminal secretory cells + basal cells. Loss of basal cell layer (proven via p63, HMWCK staining) supports invasion → prostate carcinoma

🧪 Secretion

  • Acidic, thin fluid comprising ~25% of ejaculate
  • Contents:
    • PSA (prostate-specific antigen) — serine protease that cleaves semenogelin to liquefy the coagulum; clinical biomarker for BPH/cancer surveillance
    • Acid phosphatase (prostatic acid phosphatase, PAP) — historically used as a prostate cancer marker
    • Zinc — antibacterial, supports sperm stability
    • Citrate — for sperm energy
    • Proteolytic enzymes
  • Androgen-dependent: DHT drives prostate growth and secretion

▼ 💧 Bulbourethral (Cowper's) Glands

⭐ Small pea-sized glands at the base of the penis, embedded in the urogenital diaphragm. Secrete clear mucus ("pre-ejaculate") that precedes ejaculation.

🔬 Histology & Function

  • Epithelium: simple columnar mucus-secreting cells
  • Distinctive feature: surrounded by skeletal muscle (from the urogenital diaphragm) — the classic Step 1 histology clue distinguishing bulbourethral glands from other male accessory glands
  • Secretion:
    • Alkaline mucus → neutralizes acidic urine residue in the urethra (would otherwise damage sperm)
    • Lubricates the urethra before ejaculation
    • Shields the urethra from destructive enzymes in ejaculate
  • Female homologue: Bartholin's (greater vestibular) glands
  • Embryologic origin: urogenital sinus (same as prostate)
⚡ HY — Cowper's Gland Histology:
  • "Mucus-secreting gland surrounded by skeletal muscle" = Cowper's / bulbourethral.
  • Surrounding skeletal muscle = urogenital diaphragm (external urethral sphincter).

▼ 🍆 Penis — Structure & Erectile Tissue

⭐ The penis has three parts (root, body, glans) and three erectile bodies (paired corpora cavernosa + single corpus spongiosum). Each is a meshwork of vascular sinusoids that fills with blood during erection.

🌳 Gross Structure

  • Root: fixed, attached to the body wall (ischiocavernosus + bulbospongiosus muscles)
  • Body (shaft): free portion; skin + subcutaneous dartos-like layer + deep tunica albuginea encasing erectile bodies
  • Glans: expanded distal tip; continuous with corpus spongiosum
  • Prepuce (foreskin): covers the glans; removed in circumcision; can be inflamed (balanitis) or retracted and trapped (paraphimosis)

▼

  • Sympathetic (T10–L2) → Shoot (emission + ejaculation — smooth-muscle contractions of vas/seminal vesicles/prostate)
  • Somatic (S2–S4, pudendal) → bulbospongiosus + ischiocavernosus contractions propel semen out
⚠️ TRAP: Sildenafil + Nitrates ▼
  • Sildenafil raises cGMP by inhibiting PDE5. Nitrates raise cGMP by producing NO.
  • Taken together, severe vasodilation → life-threatening hypotension, MI.
  • Classic stem: a patient on nitrates for angina takes sildenafil → presents with hypotension, dizziness, chest pain.

Testing Your Understanding — LO 3, Question 1

A 68-year-old man presents with urinary hesitancy, weak stream, and nocturia. DRE reveals a symmetrically enlarged, rubbery prostate. His PSA is mildly elevated. He is started on tamsulosin and finasteride. Explain how each drug targets a different component of the pathophysiology of the patient's condition, and why both are often used together.

Consider: which prostatic zone is enlarged, α1 receptors on smooth muscle, DHT-driven hyperplasia, 5α-reductase pharmacology

✓ Answer: BPH involves both static (bulk) and dynamic (tone) obstruction — finasteride addresses volume, tamsulosin addresses muscle tone.

BPH arises in the transition zone and causes obstruction via two mechanisms: (1) Static — bulk enlargement of glandular/stromal tissue compresses the prostatic urethra; this is DHT-driven. Finasteride (5α-reductase inhibitor) blocks testosterone → DHT conversion, shrinking volume over several months (takes time). (2) Dynamic — α1-adrenergic tone of prostatic smooth muscle + bladder neck actively constricts urine flow. Tamsulosin (α1A-selective antagonist) relaxes this smooth muscle, giving immediate symptomatic relief. Combination therapy is standard because it addresses both components. Important teaching points: finasteride artificially lowers PSA by ~50% — must be accounted for in cancer screening; side effects include sexual dysfunction and gynecomastia. Tamsulosin can cause orthostatic hypotension and intraoperative floppy iris syndrome in cataract surgery. Consider: why would you avoid combining tamsulosin with sildenafil? (Additive vasodilation → hypotension.)

Testing Your Understanding — LO 3, Question 2

A 42-year-old man presents with erectile dysfunction and infertility. Exam reveals bilateral absence of the vas deferens on palpation. ✓ Answer: Congenital Bilateral Absence of the Vas Deferens (CBAVD) — associated with CFTR mutations.

CBAVD is strongly linked to CFTR mutations — it can occur in classic cystic fibrosis (>95% of CF males are infertile from CBAVD) and also in men with milder CFTR variants who lack overt pulmonary/pancreatic CF disease. Without the vas deferens, sperm cannot reach the ejaculate → azoospermia. Because the vas deferens develops from the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct alongside the seminal vesicles, CBAVD is often accompanied by absent or hypoplastic seminal vesicles. This explains: (1) low ejaculate volume (seminal vesicles contribute ~70% of volume); (2) absent fructose (the seminal vesicles are the sole source). CFTR mutations are thought to cause fetal obstruction → atresia of the developing duct. Work-up includes CFTR genotyping, partner genetic testing, and fertility via testicular sperm extraction + IVF/ICSI. Consider: why is Leydig function preserved in these patients? (Leydig cells are in the testicular interstitium and not derived from mesonephric ducts — testosterone, LH, and secondary sex characteristics are normal.)

▼ 🏛️ Prostatic Urethra — Through the Prostate

⭐ The first segment of the urethra passes through the prostate. It receives the ejaculatory ducts (bringing sperm + seminal vesicle fluid) and multiple prostatic ducts — essentially the "confluence point" where urine and semen pathways merge.

🔬 Histology & Key Landmarks

  • Length: ~3–4 cm
  • Epithelium: transitional epithelium (urothelium) — same as the bladder
    • "Umbrella cells" at the surface allow stretching as volume changes
    • Continuous with bladder urothelium (hence shared pathology: urothelial/transitional cell carcinoma)
  • Landmarks:
    • Verumontanum (seminal colliculus) — midline posterior prominence; the ejaculatory ducts open here, flanking the prostatic utricle (a small Müllerian-duct remnant analogous to the uterus in females)
    • Multiple small openings of prostatic ducts around the verumontanum
  • Sphincter: internal urethral sphincter (smooth muscle, α1-sympathetic control) at the bladder-urethra junction — contracts during ejaculation to prevent retrograde flow into the bladder
⚡ HY — Retrograde Ejaculation:
  • Internal urethral sphincter dysfunction → ejaculate flows backward into the bladder → low-volume ejaculate, cloudy post-coital urine, infertility.
  • Causes: diabetic autonomic neuropathy, α1 blockers (tamsulosin), post-prostatectomy, post-TURP.

▼ 💪 Membranous Urethra — Through the Diaphragm

⭐ The shortest and narrowest segment — just ~1–2 cm — passing through the urogenital diaphragm. It is encircled by the external urethral sphincter (voluntary, skeletal muscle). Paired bulbourethral glands sit lateral to it.

🔬 Histology

  • Length: ~1–2 cm (shortest segment)
  • Epithelium: pseudostratified or stratified columnar
  • Sphincter: external urethral sphincter — skeletal muscle, voluntary control via somatic pudendal nerve (S2–S4) ("S2, 3, 4 keeps the penis off the floor" also applies)
  • Relations: bulbourethral (Cowper's) gland ducts open into the next segment (penile urethra), but the glands themselves sit around this section
⚡ HY — Clinical Pearls:
  • Membranous urethra is the most fixed/narrowest segment → most common site of traumatic rupture in straddle injuries + pelvic fractures (as the pelvis fractures, the urogenital diaphragm shears).
  • Urethral catheterization is more challenging here because of the external sphincter tone — a "stop, relax, advance" technique is often needed.

▼ 🍆 Penile (Spongy) Urethra — Through the Corpus Spongiosum

⭐ The longest segment (~15 cm) runs through the corpus spongiosum, which cushions it and prevents it from collapsing during erection. It terminates at the external urethral meatus on the glans.

🔬 Histology

  • Length: ~15 cm (longest segment)
  • Epithelium along its course:
    • Most of the length: pseudostratified or stratified columnar
    • Near the external meatus / fossa navicularis (dilated distal portion): stratified squamous non-keratinized (continuous with glans skin)
  • Glands of Littré: small mucus-secreting glands along the wall that lubricate during passage of ejaculate
  • Bulbourethral (Cowper's) gland ducts empty into the proximal penile urethra
  • The corpus spongiosum surrounds it — remains compressible during erection so urethra stays patent
⚡ HY — Urethral Injury Locations:
  • Bulbous urethra (proximal penile): straddle injury to perineum → blood/bruising at the perineum.
  • Membranous urethra: pelvic fracture with diastasis of the urogenital diaphragm.
  • Prostatic urethra: iatrogenic (TURP, catheter trauma).
  • Classic finding: blood at the urethral meatus after pelvic trauma → obtain retrograde urethrogram before attempting catheterization.

▼ 🚑 Clinical Correlates — Urethra in the Wild

⭐ Many Step 1-testable conditions involve the urethra. Key categories: obstruction, infection, trauma, and malignancy.

🚧 Obstruction

  • BPH — benign enlargement of transition-zone prostate compresses prostatic urethra → obstructive LUTS
  • Posterior urethral valves (PUV) — congenital mucosal flaps in male infants' prostatic urethra → severe bladder outlet obstruction, hydronephrosis, oligohydramnios (Potter sequence), often fatal without intervention
  • Urethral strictures — post-trauma, post-infection (gonococcal), iatrogenic (repeat catheterization)

💥 Trauma

  • Straddle injury → bulbous (penile) urethra rupture → perineal bruising ("butterfly" pattern)
  • Pelvic fracture → membranous urethra rupture at the urogenital diaphragm
  • Penile fracture → can involve corpus spongiosum and urethra; presents with sudden pain + "cracking" sensation during intercourse, flaccid penis with hematoma
  • Always suspect urethral injury with: blood at meatus, high-riding prostate on DRE, perineal bruising, inability to void, hematuria
  • Work-up: retrograde urethrogram BEFORE attempting any catheterization

🎗️ Malignancy

  • Proximal urethra (prostatic): urothelial (transitional) cell carcinoma — matches its epithelium
  • Distal urethra (penile, near meatus): squamous cell carcinoma — matches the squamous epithelium there
  • This "epithelium predicts histology" rule is a Step 1 favorite
📚 Step 1 Summary
  • Three segments: Prostatic (transitional) → Membranous (pseudostrat/strat columnar; external sphincter here) → Penile/Spongy (pseudostrat/strat columnar; squamous at meatus).
  • Internal sphincter = smooth muscle, α1, involuntary — prevents retrograde ejaculation.
  • External sphincter = skeletal muscle, pudendal (S2–S4), voluntary — urinary continence.
  • Epithelium predicts malignancy: proximal = urothelial CA, distal = squamous CA.
  • Straddle trauma = bulbous urethra; pelvic fracture = membranous urethra.
  • Retrograde urethrogram before catheter if urethral injury suspected.

Testing Your Understanding — LO 4, Question 1

A 72-year-old man with diabetes and BPH on tamsulosin reports that during intercourse he now has a barely perceptible ejaculate volume, but his post-coital urine is cloudy. Explain the underlying urethral and autonomic physiology, and why tamsulosin contributes to the problem.

Consider: internal urethral sphincter, sympathetic α1 tone, ejaculatory duct opening, mechanical vs. neural dysfunction

✓ Answer: Retrograde ejaculation — internal urethral sphincter fails to close → semen enters the bladder.

Normally, during the emission phase of ejaculation, sympathetic α1-adrenergic tone closes the internal urethral sphincter at the bladder neck, ensuring that semen released into the prostatic urethra is propelled forward through the external meatus rather than backward into the bladder. Tamsulosin is a selective α1A-antagonist used to relax prostatic and bladder-neck smooth muscle in BPH — but it also disables the internal sphincter, causing retrograde ejaculation. Additive causes in this patient: diabetic autonomic neuropathy further impairs sympathetic function. Clinical findings: low-volume or anejaculation, orgasm preserved, post-coital urinalysis reveals sperm in urine (confirming the diagnosis). Management: if fertility is not desired, no treatment needed; if desired, switch off α1 blockers or use sympathomimetics (imipramine, pseudoephedrine) or perform sperm retrieval from post-orgasm urine for IVF/ICSI. Consider: why does tamsulosin NOT cause urinary incontinence? (The external urethral sphincter — skeletal muscle controlled by the pudendal nerve, independent of α1 tone.)

A 30-year-old man presents to the ED after a high-speed motorcycle accident with a displaced pelvic fracture. He is hemodynamically stable, unable to void, and has blood at the urethral meatus. ✓ Answer: Obtain a retrograde urethrogram before attempting catheterization — pelvic fracture classically disrupts the membranous urethra.

In a pelvic fracture with diastasis of the pubic symphysis, the shearing forces disrupt the diaphragm and commonly rupture the membranous urethra. A. Leydig cells

B. Sertoli cells and germ cells within the seminiferous tubules

C. Anterior pituitary gonadotroph cells

D. Hypothalamic GnRH-producing neurons

E. Prostate gland

✓ Correct: B — Sertoli cells and germ cells (seminiferous tubule damage).

The hormonal pattern — normal testosterone + LH, elevated FSH, and low inhibin B — is classic for isolated Sertoli/germ cell damage. Mumps orchitis disrupts the blood-testis barrier and damages the thermally and immunologically sensitive seminiferous epithelium. Question 2

A researcher performs electron microscopy on a cross-section of a seminiferous tubule. She identifies tight junctions between adjacent support cells, dividing the seminiferous epithelium into two compartments: a basal compartment containing mitotic cells and an adluminal compartment containing meiotic and post-meiotic cells. Which of the following best explains the physiologic purpose of this barrier?

A. To prevent bacterial translocation from the bloodstream

B. To sequester meiotic germ cells expressing novel surface antigens from immune surveillance

C. To concentrate FSH within the seminiferous lumen

D. To block testosterone diffusion into the tubule

E. To prevent sperm from entering the ductus epididymis prematurely

✓ Correct: B — Blood-testis barrier sequesters meiotic germ cells from the immune system.

The blood-testis barrier is formed by Sertoli-Sertoli tight junctions. Meiotic and post-meiotic germ cells express unique antigens (e.g., sperm-specific proteins) that the immune system never encountered during fetal development when self-tolerance was established. Without the barrier, these cells would be attacked → autoimmune orchitis + infertility. The barrier also creates a specialized microenvironment with ~100× higher testosterone (via Sertoli-secreted ABP), needed for spermatogenesis. A — the barrier isn't primarily antibacterial. Question 3

A 19-year-old bodybuilder has been taking high-dose anabolic steroids for 18 months. On exam, both testes appear small and soft. He complains of infertility and gynecomastia. Labs: testosterone 950 ng/dL (high; exogenous), LH 1.2 mIU/mL (low), FSH 0.8 mIU/mL (low), estradiol 60 pg/mL (high). Which of the following best explains the testicular atrophy and infertility?

A. Leydig cell hyperplasia from elevated LH

B. Exogenous testosterone suppresses GnRH/LH/FSH → loss of intratesticular testosterone and FSH support

C. Direct anabolic steroid toxicity on germ cells

D. Autoimmune destruction of seminiferous tubules

E. Anti-sperm antibody formation from an abnormal blood-testis barrier

✓ Correct: B — Exogenous testosterone negatively feeds back on the hypothalamus/pituitary, suppressing LH and FSH → testicular atrophy and infertility.

Although serum testosterone is elevated (from the exogenous source), the intratesticular testosterone concentration drops dramatically because Leydig cells are no longer being stimulated by LH. Spermatogenesis requires very high intratesticular testosterone (~100× plasma levels, maintained by Sertoli-cell ABP and LH-driven Leydig production). Without endogenous Leydig stimulation + FSH support of Sertoli cells, spermatogenesis ceases → infertility. The unused Leydig cells atrophy. Similar pattern seen with any exogenous androgen abuse, transgender hormone therapy, or prostate cancer androgen-deprivation treatment. A 4-year-old boy has chronic recurrent sinus and lung infections, was found to have dextrocardia on a preoperative chest X-ray, and now his parents are told he may have infertility when he grows up. A defect in which subcellular structure explains all of these findings?

B. 9+2 microtubule axoneme / dynein arm

C. Actin-myosin crossbridges

D. Gap junction connexins

E. Intermediate filaments

✓ Correct: B — Dynein arm defect in the 9+2 axoneme (Kartagener syndrome / primary ciliary dyskinesia).

Kartagener syndrome (PCD) is caused by mutations affecting dynein arms of the 9+2 axoneme, which is the core motility apparatus of cilia AND sperm flagella (identical structure). The clinical triad: (1) situs inversus or heterotaxy (because ciliary beating of the embryonic node establishes L–R asymmetry during gastrulation); (2) chronic sinusitis + bronchiectasis (immotile respiratory cilia → mucus stasis → recurrent infection); (3) infertility in males (immotile sperm) and reduced fertility in females (reduced oviduct ciliary transport). ✓ Correct: D — Ductus epididymis (pseudostratified columnar with stereocilia + thin muscle).

The combination of pseudostratified columnar epithelium with long non-motile apical projections (stereocilia), a lumen full of mature sperm, and a thin smooth-muscle coat is diagnostic of the ductus epididymis. Question 10

A 55-year-old man on nitrates for stable angina takes a tablet of sildenafil before intercourse. He becomes severely hypotensive and develops chest pain. What is the mechanism of the drug interaction?

A. Additive effects on guanylate cyclase → cGMP accumulation → excessive vasodilation

B. Competitive antagonism at β1 receptors → bradycardia

C. Inhibition of angiotensin-converting enzyme → loss of vasopressor tone

D. Blockade of calcium channels → decreased cardiac output

E. Direct myocardial toxicity from sildenafil

✓ Correct: A — Both drugs increase cGMP (nitrates via NO → guanylate cyclase; sildenafil via PDE5 inhibition) → additive, severe vasodilation.

During erection, parasympathetic nerves release NO → activates guanylate cyclase → ↑ cGMP → smooth-muscle relaxation → penile vasodilation. Phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) normally degrades cGMP. Teaching points: always screen for nitrate use before prescribing PDE5 inhibitors; they can be used 24 h after last nitrate dose (48 h for tadalafil due to longer half-life).
